Chapter 1 mentioned, in passing, that "flashbulb memories" — the vivid, detailed memories people carry of exactly where they were during a major public event — feel uniquely accurate but aren't. This deserves closer attention, because flashbulb memories are the single strongest real-world case where confidence and accuracy come apart, and the research behind that gap is more specific, and stranger, than a passing mention can capture.

Why these memories feel different from ordinary ones

Psychologists Roger Brown and James Kulik coined the term flashbulb memory in 1977, describing the striking, photograph-like vividness that memories of shocking, emotionally significant public events (a president's assassination, a disaster, a terrorist attack) seem to carry — full of specific, seemingly preserved sensory detail: what room you were in, what you were wearing, who told you, what the light looked like. Brown and Kulik's original theory proposed a special neurological "flashbulb" mechanism, printing these particular memories with a kind of permanence ordinary memories don't get. That original theory — a genuinely different, more reliable encoding process for high-emotion events — has not held up well against the data collected since.

Neisser and the Challenger disaster: catching the drift in the act

Psychologist Ulric Neisser ran the study that first broke the "special mechanism" theory decisively. The day after the Space Shuttle Challenger exploded in 1986, Neisser had students write down, in detail, exactly how they'd heard the news. Two and a half years later, he asked the same students to recall the same moment again — and compared the two accounts directly. The results were stark: roughly a third of the follow-up accounts were substantially wrong on major details (who told them, where they were, what they were doing), and some were wrong on nearly every detail checked. Critically, the students' confidence in their later, inaccurate accounts was just as high as their confidence in the original, accurate ones — several insisted, when shown their own handwriting from the day after the event, that the earlier account must be the mistaken one, not their current, vivid memory.

September 11th: the same pattern, at a larger scale

Later studies replicated Neisser's basic finding using the September 11, 2001 attacks, surveying thousands of people within days of the event and again at one, three, and ten years afterward. The pattern held at scale: substantial, measurable memory decay and inconsistency over time, especially in peripheral details, while people's confidence in their memories stayed remarkably stable and high throughout the entire ten-year period — the confidence line and the accuracy line, plotted together, don't track each other at all; one stays essentially flat while the other visibly declines. What does appear genuinely special about flashbulb memories, compared to ordinary ones, isn't accuracy — it's the vividness and the confidence, both of which seem to get frozen in place independent of whether the actual content is drifting underneath them.

Why this specific finding matters beyond trivia about famous events

The flashbulb memory research matters well past its original, narrow subject because it isolates one variable so cleanly: these are memories people had every reason to expect would be reliable — high emotional stakes, extensive rehearsal through retelling, immense personal and cultural significance — and the data shows accuracy declining anyway, right alongside supreme, unwavering confidence. If the memory type most primed to feel bulletproof still degrades this predictably while confidence stays fixed, the same gap almost certainly applies, probably more severely, to the vast majority of ordinary memories that never get the benefit of a flashbulb's vividness or repeated retelling at all — Chapter 1's broader argument, sharpened to its most convincing single case.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Pick your own flashbulb memory of a major public or personal event and try to recall it in detail — then, if possible, check it against something you wrote or said about it closer to the time. Notice any drift, and notice your confidence level regardless of what you find.
  • Next time you're absolutely certain about the details of a vivid memory, remember that flashbulb memory research found the same certainty in memories that turned out to be substantially wrong — a useful, humbling benchmark.
