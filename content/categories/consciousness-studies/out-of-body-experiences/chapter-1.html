Reports of "leaving the body" — a vivid sensation of floating above or outside yourself, sometimes looking down at your own physical form — show up across cultures, centuries, and belief systems, usually called astral projection, an out-of-body experience, or simply an OBE. For a long time this sat firmly in the domain of the unprovable: a subjective report with no way to check it against anything external. Then, in 2002, neurosurgeons stimulating a specific brain region during unrelated epilepsy treatment accidentally triggered the exact sensation on demand.

The temporoparietal junction

Neurologist Olaf Blanke was mapping a patient's brain with electrical stimulation, standard practice before epilepsy surgery, when stimulating a region called the temporoparietal junction (TPJ — a brain region, near the back and side of the head, involved in combining signals from vision, balance, and touch into one coherent sense of where your body is) caused the patient to report suddenly seeing herself lying on the bed below, from a vantage point near the ceiling. Stop the stimulation, and the sensation stopped immediately. Blanke's explanation: the TPJ's normal job is fusing multiple streams of bodily sensation into a single, located "self" — and when that fusion process is disrupted, one plausible result is a self that feels briefly located somewhere other than where the physical body actually is.

What this settles, and what it doesn't

This finding settles one specific question convincingly: the felt experience of an OBE is a genuine, physically triggerable brain event, not fabrication or pure suggestion — it can be produced on demand, repeatedly, in a controlled setting. It does not settle the much bigger question that gives "astral projection" its name: whether anything actually leaves the body during a naturally occurring OBE, or whether the entire experience — including any information a person believes they perceived while "outside" themselves — is generated entirely inside a still-embodied brain. Every rigorously controlled test of the strong claim (can an OBE genuinely perceive information, like a hidden number placed high on a shelf, that would be inaccessible to the physical body?) has failed to find evidence for it. The felt sensation of floating and viewing your body is real and explainable; verified perception from outside the body has never been demonstrated under controlled conditions.

Susan Blackmore: a rare case of a believer turning into a scientist

Parapsychologist Susan Blackmore is an unusually credible voice in this specific territory because of her own history: she had spontaneous OBEs herself as a young researcher, initially believed they were evidence of a separable "astral body," and spent a career afterward actually testing that belief under controlled conditions — findings that repeatedly failed to support it. Her book Dying to Live lays out both the genuine experience and the evidence trail that changed her mind, without dismissing why the experience itself feels so convincingly real to the people who have it.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Notice the general pattern: next time you hear a personal account of an "unexplainable" experience, try separating two different questions — is the experience itself real (almost always yes), and is the explanation the person is offering for it actually the best available one (a separate, harder question)?
  • Try connecting it to the sleep-paralysis link: many spontaneous OBE reports happen right at the edge of sleep — notice, if you've ever had a strange sensation while falling asleep or waking up, whether it fits that specific transitional window rather than full wakefulness.
