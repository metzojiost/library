Chapter 2: Why Capital Income Compounds Faster Than Labor Income

~3 min read

Chapter 1 split leverage into four types and left one thread open: why does capital income tend to compound faster than labor income over time, even for equally hardworking, equally skilled people? This chapter picks that up directly — it's one of the most consequential, and most misunderstood, patterns in the whole subject.

The pattern: r > g

Economist Thomas Piketty's 2013 book Capital in the Twenty-First Century compiled centuries of data across many countries and found a persistent pattern: the rate of return on capital (r — roughly, what invested wealth earns) has historically exceeded the growth rate of the overall economy (g — roughly, what wages and incomes grow by on average). When r > g, wealth that's already invested grows faster than the economy as a whole, which means, left unchecked over long periods, capital income pulls ahead of labor income as a share of total output — not because capital owners are working harder, but because compounding at a higher rate simply outpaces compounding at a lower one, and the gap widens every year it continues.

Why this isn't really about being "rich" — it's about the ratio

The mechanism doesn't require anyone to be already wealthy to matter for them personally. A person who buys an index fund with 10% of each paycheck, starting at 22, and a person who spends 100% of each paycheck and starts investing at 45, aren't just "a bit behind" each other by the years-difference — the twenty-three extra years of compounding at r produce a dramatically larger effect than the raw time gap suggests, because each year's growth compounds on top of the previous years' growth, not just on the original amount. The practical takeaway isn't "get rich to benefit from this" — it's that the r > g gap rewards starting early and staying invested specifically, independent of how much is actually being invested at first.

The honest limits of this pattern

Piketty's r > g finding is genuinely well-documented as a long-run historical pattern, but it isn't a fixed law of nature — it's held more strongly in some periods (extending, notably, through most of the 19th century and again since roughly the 1980s) and less strongly in others (the mid-20th century, particularly after the World Wars, saw the gap shrink substantially, partly through capital destruction and partly through policy). It's also a statement about long-run averages, not a guarantee for any individual investment, which can lose value entirely over shorter horizons — the pattern describes decades-long tendencies in diversified capital broadly, not a promise about any specific asset.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Run the actual numbers on your own situation: even a small, consistent share of income redirected from pure labor income into some form of capital (savings, investment, an owned asset) starts compounding from the moment it begins — the exact percentage matters far less than the decision to start the clock running at all.
