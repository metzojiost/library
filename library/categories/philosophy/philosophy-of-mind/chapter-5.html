Chapter 5: Panpsychism and Integrated Information Theory

~5 min read

Every position covered so far shares one assumption: consciousness is rare and special, something that switches on only once matter gets arranged into something as complicated as a brain — dualism, physicalism, and functionalism all disagree about the mechanism, but not about that starting point. This final chapter covers the view that rejects the assumption itself: what if experience isn't a late, rare arrival that complexity eventually produces, but something basic, present in some form all the way down?

Panpsychism: consciousness as a basic ingredient, not a late arrival

Panpsychism (from Greek "pan," all, and "psyche," mind — the view that some form of experience, however minimal, is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of physical reality, not something that only emerges in complex brains) has a genuinely old philosophical lineage, but its most influential modern defense comes from philosopher Galen Strawson, who argues physicalists have the hard problem backward. If you accept, as most physicalists do, that experience is real and that it's made of physical stuff, then physical stuff must already be capable of experience at some level — the alternative, Strawson argues, is the much stranger claim that experience can arise from something that has absolutely none of it to begin with, out of pure non-experiential matter, which he calls "radical emergence" and treats as barely coherent. Panpsychism's proposal is that a proton or an electron might have some unimaginably minimal form of proto-experience — nothing like human consciousness, no thoughts or images, just some faint, basic something-it-is-like-ness — and that your rich, unified experience is what happens when enough of that gets organized the right way.

A working theory built on a similar idea: Integrated Information Theory

Neuroscientist Giulio Tononi developed a more formal, quantifiable version of a related idea starting in 2004, called Integrated Information Theory (IIT — a theory proposing that consciousness corresponds to a system's capacity to integrate information in a way that can't be broken down into independent parts). IIT defines a measurable quantity, Φ (phi — a mathematical measure of how much a system's information is integrated, as opposed to just the sum of its separate parts), and proposes that the amount of Φ a physical system has corresponds directly to how conscious it is. A system where the whole genuinely does more than the sum of independently-acting parts has high Φ; a system that's just a pile of parts running in parallel with little real interaction has low Φ, even if it's processing a huge amount of information. IIT makes a real, checkable prediction that lines up with an odd anatomical fact: your cerebellum has more individual neurons than your entire cerebral cortex, yet damage to it barely touches conscious experience, while comparatively small cortical damage can devastate it. IIT explains this by pointing to architecture — the cerebellum is wired as many small, largely independent circuits, giving it low Φ despite its neuron count, while the cortex is densely, richly interconnected, giving it high Φ despite having fewer cells.

Two honest problems neither view has solved

Panpsychism's own advocates acknowledge its central unsolved difficulty, called the combination problem: even granting that a proton might have some minimal proto-experience, it's genuinely unclear how billions of those micro-experiences could combine into one unified experience like yours, rather than remaining billions of separate, tiny experiences that never add up to a single "you." Simply asserting that they combine doesn't explain the mechanism, and no panpsychist has produced one that's widely accepted. IIT has its own well-known problem: because Φ is defined mathematically from a system's causal structure alone, critics — most notably computer scientist Scott Aaronson in 2014 — have pointed out that certain simple, purpose-built grid-like networks with no plausible claim to any inner life at all can be constructed to have extremely high Φ, mathematically higher than a human brain's, which strikes most people as an absurd result for a theory of consciousness to produce. Tononi's response is that IIT should be trusted over the intuition in genuinely novel cases — but that's a difficult position to defend when the entire theory's credibility rests on matching intuitions everywhere else, like the cerebellum case above.

Five chapters, no resolution — and that's the honest ending

Dualism can't explain how a non-physical mind pushes physical neurons around. Physicalism and functionalism can't close the hard problem's gap between mechanism and felt experience. The Chinese Room casts doubt on whether symbol manipulation, however sophisticated, is ever enough on its own. Panpsychism and IIT offer a genuinely different starting point, and inherit genuinely different unsolved problems of their own. This isn't a failure of the topic — it's an accurate report of where the actual state of the art sits: consciousness remains the one thing every human has the most direct, undeniable access to, and simultaneously the thing philosophy and neuroscience together still can't fully explain.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Sit with Strawson's "radical emergence" objection for a minute: does it strike you as more or less strange than the idea that a proton might have some faint trace of experience? Notice which discomfort feels bigger to you, and why.
  • Revisit your own answer from Chapter 4's "try this" about what would settle machine consciousness — now run it through IIT: would your test actually distinguish high integration from a system that's merely processing a lot of information in parallel?
