Four chapters have covered ways groups make individual behavior worse — diffused responsibility, diluted effort, manufactured hostility, amplified norms. This closing chapter covers the genuine exception: specific, well-documented conditions under which combining many people's independent input produces an answer more accurate than any individual expert could reach alone — and, just as importantly, exactly what has to be true for that to actually happen, since the same aggregation mechanism can produce the opposite result when those conditions are missing.

Galton's ox, and the number nobody guessed but everybody found together

The founding demonstration comes from statistician Francis Galton, who in 1906 observed a weight-guessing competition at a country fair, where roughly 800 attendees each guessed the weight of an ox after it was slaughtered and dressed. Galton, skeptical that an uneducated crowd could estimate anything accurately, collected all 800 guesses afterward specifically to prove the point — and found the average of all the guesses came within 1% of the ox's actual weight, closer than the estimate of any individual expert judge who had also guessed. No single attendee had needed to be particularly accurate; the errors in individual guesses, running in different directions and different magnitudes for different reasons, canceled out in aggregate, leaving the underlying, shared signal intact. This is the same statistical logic behind Chapter 2's earlier library material on averaging independent judgments to reduce noise, now demonstrated on a crowd scale rather than a small panel of experts.

The conditions that actually have to hold

Journalist James Surowiecki's 2004 book The Wisdom of Crowds systematized what separates a genuinely wise crowd from the ordinary group failures covered in the previous four chapters, identifying specific, checkable conditions rather than treating "crowds are wise" as a general law. Diversity of opinion requires each person to have some independent source of information or private judgment, even if imperfect. Independence requires that people's opinions not be determined by, or unduly influenced by, the opinions of others around them — precisely the condition Chapter 2's independent-aggregation discussion already flagged, and precisely the condition Chapters 3 and 4's group conformity and norm-amplification effects directly violate. Decentralization requires people to be able to draw on local, specialized knowledge rather than one central source. And aggregation requires some genuine mechanism — an average, a market price, a vote — for actually combining individual judgments into a collective one, rather than simply letting the loudest or most confident voice dominate the group's stated final answer.

Why this chapter isn't actually a contradiction of the previous four

Read correctly, this chapter doesn't undercut anything Chapters 1 through 4 established — it identifies the precise conditions under which crowds avoid exactly those failures. Independence is the load-bearing condition, and it's precisely what breaks down in every failure mode this topic has covered: social loafing (Chapter 2) exploits shared, blended effort rather than requiring independent contribution; Robbers Cave-style intergroup conflict (Chapter 3) is driven by strong shared in-group identity actively overriding independent individual judgment; and SIDE's group-norm amplification (Chapter 4) is, by definition, individual judgment being replaced by a locally dominant, shared group norm. A "wise crowd" in Surowiecki's sense isn't really a crowd behaving as a unified group at all — it's a large number of people functioning as independent, separately-informed data points that happen to be aggregated afterward, which is closer to Chapter 2's blindfolded, individually-measured shouting than to Chapter 3's tightly bonded, identity-driven Eagles and Rattlers.

Recognizing which situation you're actually in

This gives the whole topic a genuinely practical closing test: before trusting a crowd's collective judgment — a prediction market, a large survey, an average of many opinions — check Surowiecki's four conditions directly rather than assuming size alone confers wisdom. Were the contributing judgments genuinely independent, or had people already seen and been influenced by each other's answers before answering (in which case you likely have amplified conformity, not aggregated wisdom)? Was there real diversity of information behind the guesses, or was everyone drawing on the identical single source? A crowd that fails these conditions isn't a smaller, weaker version of Galton's ox-guessers — it's functionally the deindividuated, norm-amplified, conformity-driven crowd this entire topic spent four chapters warning about, wearing a different name.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Next time you're relying on a crowd's collective opinion — reviews, a poll, a prediction market — check Surowiecki's four conditions directly before trusting the aggregate as genuinely wise rather than merely popular.
  • Try Galton's demonstration yourself: ask a group of people to independently, privately estimate something (the number of items in a jar, an unfamiliar quantity) without conferring first, then average the guesses and compare to any individual guess.
