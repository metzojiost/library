Chapter 1 ended with a hook worth taking seriously: John Bowlby's claim that the bond between infant and caregiver is a primary biological need in its own right, not just a byproduct of feeding, was considered radical enough to be dismissed for years by a psychiatric establishment that assumed the opposite — that a baby loves its mother mainly because she's the source of food, an idea sometimes called "cupboard love" theory. The experiment that most directly demolished that assumption wasn't run on humans at all. It was run on baby rhesus monkeys, and it's remembered as one of psychology's most visually striking, and most ethically troubling, studies.

Two fake mothers, one made of wire

Psychologist Harry Harlow, working in the 1950s, separated infant rhesus monkeys from their real mothers and raised them instead with two artificial substitutes. One was a bare wire frame fitted with a bottle, providing milk — the "cupboard love" theory's entire prediction in physical form. The other was a similar frame wrapped in soft terrycloth, providing no food at all, just a comforting surface to cling to. If attachment really was just about food, the infants should have bonded with the wire mother that fed them. They didn't. The infant monkeys spent the overwhelming majority of their time clinging to the soft cloth mother, visiting the wire mother only briefly to feed before hurrying back to the cloth one for comfort — and when Harlow introduced something frightening into the cage, the infants ran straight to the cloth mother for reassurance, using her as exactly the kind of secure base Chapter 1 described in Ainsworth's later work, regardless of which mother had ever fed them.

What happened when comfort was entirely absent

Harlow's further, more ethically troubling experiments (raising monkeys with no cloth mother at all, or in total isolation) produced monkeys with severe, lasting social and emotional dysfunction as adults — unusually aggressive or fearful, unable to mate normally, and in the case of females who did become mothers, sometimes neglectful or abusive toward their own infants, having had no model of the interaction themselves. These experiments are now widely regarded as having crossed real ethical lines by modern research standards, and they're taught today as much for that historical lesson as for their scientific content — but the core scientific finding they helped establish was hard to dispute: contact comfort, not food, was the actual driver of the attachment bond, and its absence in early life produced damage that simple nutrition and physical care couldn't prevent.

How this reframed Bowlby's theory from speculation to biology

Harlow's monkey studies gave Bowlby's still-developing ideas about attachment an experimental foundation the psychiatric establishment couldn't easily wave away, since it didn't rely on interpreting children's behavior through a psychoanalytic lens (the dominant framework at the time) at all — it was a stark, physical demonstration in a closely related primate species. Bowlby went on to argue, drawing on this and his own extensive observations of hospitalized and institutionalized children, that attachment behavior (crying, clinging, following, seeking proximity to a caregiver when frightened) is an evolved system, shaped by natural selection specifically because infants who stayed close to a protective caregiver survived at higher rates than infants who didn't — putting attachment on the same biological footing as hunger or fear, rather than treating it as a secondary emotional attachment that grew out of feeding.

Why this still matters for how caregiving gets understood today

The practical legacy of this research runs directly into modern parenting and childcare guidance: physical comfort, responsiveness, and consistent presence are now understood as core developmental needs in their own right, not as indulgences layered on top of meeting a baby's "real" physical needs. Institutional childcare practices that once prioritized efficient physical care over consistent, responsive human contact — common in some mid-20th-century orphanages and hospitals — have been directly linked, through later research building on Harlow and Bowlby's foundational work, to the kind of developmental harm Harlow's isolated monkeys showed in more extreme form. The wire mother, in other words, turned out to be a genuinely useful cautionary image for an entire era of institutional care that hadn't yet understood what Harlow's cloth mother was demonstrating.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Notice "cupboard love" assumptions in how care gets talked about around you — the idea that meeting practical needs (food, shelter, money) should be enough on its own, without physical presence or comfort — and consider where Harlow's finding pushes back on that.
  • Think about a relationship in your own life where comfort, not practical usefulness, is clearly the actual bond — Harlow's monkeys picked comfort every time it was available, regardless of who fed them.
