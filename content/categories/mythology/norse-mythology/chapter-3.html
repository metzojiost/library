Chapter 1 introduced Jotunheim, the giants' realm, mostly as a name on the map of Yggdrasil. But the relationship between the gods and the giants — constant, often violent, occasionally cooperative, and genuinely central to nearly every major myth — deserves its own chapter, and there's no better guide through it than Thor, the god most directly, repeatedly involved with them.

Thor's actual role: not a symbol, a bouncer

Thor, god of thunder and son of Odin, is often flattened in modern retellings into a generic strongman, but his functional role in the mythology is more specific: he's the gods' primary defense against the giants, constantly patrolling the border between order (Asgard, Midgard) and the chaotic forces the giants represent. His hammer, Mjölnir, forged by dwarven craftsmen, always returns to his hand after being thrown — making him less a wandering hero seeking adventure and more a standing, reliable defense mechanism the other gods depend on, deployed again and again whenever a giant threat escalates.

A myth that plays the tension for comedy: Thrymskvida

Not every giant myth is grim. In the poem Þrymskviða (Thrymskvida), the giant Thrym steals Mjölnir and demands the goddess Freyja as his bride in exchange for its return. Rather than accept, the gods send Thor himself, disguised as Freyja in a bridal veil, with Loki posing as his handmaiden, to infiltrate the giants' wedding feast and steal the hammer back. Thor, still in disguise, reportedly eats an entire ox and eight salmon at the feast and drinks three casks of mead, alarming the giants — Loki quickly improvises an excuse (the "bride" was simply too excited to eat or sleep for over a week). The moment Mjölnir is finally brought out to bless the marriage, Thor grabs it and kills every giant at the wedding. The story is played almost entirely for comic absurdity rather than grandeur — a useful reminder that Norse myth wasn't uniformly solemn; audiences clearly enjoyed watching their most powerful god humiliated in a dress before violently reasserting himself.

Fishing for the end of the world, early

A darker, more consequential myth has Thor going fishing with the giant Hymir, using an ox's head as bait — and hooking Jörmungandr, the monstrous World Serpent, a child of Loki grown so enormous it encircles the entirety of Midgard, gripping its own tail in its mouth. Thor hauls the serpent up from the ocean depths, close enough to strike a killing blow — and in most surviving versions, the terrified giant Hymir cuts the fishing line at the last second, letting Jörmungandr sink back into the sea. Thor doesn't get to finish the kill. This matters because Norse myth already tells you exactly when he will: Ragnarok's prophecy specifically names Thor and Jörmungandr as destined to kill each other in their final confrontation, with Thor managing to slay the serpent but dying moments later from its venom. The fishing trip isn't a stand-alone adventure — it's a rehearsal for an ending the audience already knows is coming, adding real dramatic weight to what could otherwise read as just another monster story.

Not every giant is an enemy

It's worth resisting the temptation to read "giant" as simply meaning "monster" — the actual mythology is messier and more interesting than that. Several of the gods themselves have giant ancestry (Odin's own mother was a giantess), gods and giants intermarry across multiple stories, and giants are sometimes sources of essential wisdom the gods actively seek out — in one myth, Odin travels to the giant Vafthrudnir specifically to win a contest of cosmological knowledge. The relationship is less a clean war between good and evil than an uneasy, constantly negotiated boundary between two closely related but structurally opposed kinds of power — which fits the broader theme Chapter 1 already established: a mythology far more interested in irresolvable tension than in tidy moral binaries.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Notice how differently Thrymskvida and the Jörmungandr myth handle the same core relationship (Thor versus a giant threat) — one played almost entirely as comedy, the other loaded with foreshadowed tragedy — and consider what that range suggests about the mythology's actual tone versus its modern, uniformly-epic reputation.
  • Try mapping "giant" in your own life onto something less binary than monster-versus-hero — a force, person, or situation that's genuinely oppositional but not simply evil, the way the giants function here.
