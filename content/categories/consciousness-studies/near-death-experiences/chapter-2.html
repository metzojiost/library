Chapter 1's frequency chart listed the life review among the commonly reported elements of a near-death experience — a rapid, often startlingly vivid re-experiencing of major moments from one's entire life, frequently described as happening all at once or outside normal time. This specific element deserves its own chapter, because it has one of the more concrete, physiologically grounded candidate explanations of any single NDE component, discovered in a genuinely unusual place: a study on dying rats.

The Borjigin surge-of-gamma finding

Neuroscientist Jimo Borjigin and colleagues published a striking 2013 study measuring EEG activity in rats during induced cardiac arrest, and found something that directly complicated the assumption that a dying brain simply, gradually shuts down. In the roughly 30 seconds immediately following cardiac arrest, before all measurable brain activity ceased entirely, the rats showed a sharp, transient surge in gamma wave activity — the same high-frequency band associated, in the meditation research covered elsewhere in this library, with binding together information from different brain regions into unified moments of experience — with this surge actually exceeding the gamma levels measured during the rats' normal, fully conscious waking state. This was an unexpected, striking result: rather than a smooth decline into nothing, the dying brain, at least in this rodent model, appeared to produce a brief, intense burst of exactly the kind of coordinated neural activity associated with rich, integrated conscious experience.

Why this offers a plausible mechanism for the life review specifically

Borjigin's finding gives a genuinely specific, mechanistically grounded candidate explanation for the life review element in particular, distinct from the more general explanations (REM intrusion, oxygen deprivation) Chapter 1 already covered for NDEs broadly. If a similar surge occurs in the human brain during the process of dying — a claim that has since found some corroborating evidence in a small number of human EEG cases recorded incidentally during end-of-life care, though this remains a much smaller and harder-to-obtain dataset than the rat studies — a brief, intense burst of highly coordinated activity, potentially recruiting memory-related brain regions in an unusual, compressed way, could plausibly produce exactly the kind of rapid, vivid, and unusually integrated recall the life review describes, compressed into a subjectively vast span of experienced time relative to the brief span of actual brain activity producing it.

Why this doesn't settle the question, even for this one specific element

Consistent with this topic's running commitment to precision about what evidence does and doesn't establish, it's worth being direct about the real limits here. The foundational finding is from rats, and translating any rodent neuroscience finding directly to human subjective experience carries real, well-known uncertainty — a rat's brain producing a gamma surge doesn't, on its own, demonstrate that a human brain in the same situation experiences anything like a structured, meaningful life review, only that the raw neural conditions that might support something like rich experience are present. The human EEG corroboration that does exist is drawn from a small number of opportunistic recordings in clinical settings, not a large, controlled study, and correlating a physiological surge with a specific, richly detailed subjective report (which can only be collected after the fact, from survivors, subject to all of Chapter 1's memory-reconstruction concerns covered elsewhere in this library) remains genuinely difficult to do rigorously.

What this chapter actually adds to the topic

What the Borjigin finding contributes isn't proof that the life review has been fully explained — it's a genuine, physiologically concrete existence proof that the dying brain is not simply, uniformly shutting down in the passive way it's often assumed to, and that a specific, testable mechanism exists that could plausibly generate rich, bound, memory-laden experience in exactly the brief window many NDEs are reported from. This adds real, specific weight to the physiological side of Chapter 1's still-open debate, without fully closing it — precisely the kind of incremental, honestly-bounded progress this entire topic keeps returning to rather than a single decisive answer in either direction.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Notice the gap between "a plausible mechanism exists" and "the phenomenon has been fully explained" — the Borjigin finding is genuine evidence, not a closed case, and it's worth holding that distinction precisely.
  • If you know someone who's had a life-review-type NDE, and it feels appropriate to ask, notice how they describe the subjective time compression — comparing it to this chapter's brief-but-intense physiological window is a genuinely interesting exercise, without needing to resolve the deeper question either way.
