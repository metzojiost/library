Every chapter so far has assumed something largely uncontroversial: that a published, peer-reviewed scientific finding is at least a trustworthy starting point, even while debating how to interpret its philosophical implications. Starting around 2011, a wave of research began seriously undermining that assumption for a specific, large swath of published science — not by attacking any particular theory, but by attacking whether many published results were ever really findings at all.

The Reproducibility Project: trying to redo 100 studies

In 2015, a large collaboration of over 270 researchers, the Open Science Collaboration, attempted to directly replicate 100 psychology studies previously published in top journals — running the same experiments again, as closely as possible to the originals, with adequate sample sizes. The result: only about 36% of the replications reproduced a statistically significant effect in the same direction as the original study, and even successful replications typically found weaker effects than first reported. Similar, though not identical, patterns of low replication rates turned up in subsequent large-scale efforts in cancer biology, economics, and other fields. This became known as the replication crisis (the widespread finding, starting in the early 2010s, that a large share of published, peer-reviewed research findings fail to reproduce when the same study is run again independently), and it forced a serious reckoning with how "settled science" actually gets produced.

How this happened without anyone necessarily committing fraud

The uncomfortable finding underneath the replication crisis is that most of it wasn't caused by deliberate cheating — it was caused by ordinary, widespread research practices that happened to reliably inflate false positives. P-hacking (running many different analyses, variable combinations, or subgroup comparisons on the same data until one happens to cross the conventional statistical significance threshold, then reporting only that one) lets a researcher find "something significant" in almost any sufficiently large dataset, purely by chance, without any real underlying effect. Publication bias compounds this from the outside: journals have historically been far more willing to publish a surprising, positive finding than a boring null result, so even if ten labs run the same experiment and only one gets a positive result by pure chance, that one positive result is the one that gets published — the nine null results mostly vanish into file drawers, and no reader ever sees the full picture. Neither practice requires any individual bad actor; both emerge naturally from ordinary incentives (a paper is easier to publish, and a career easier to build, on positive findings) interacting with weak methodological safeguards.

Connecting this back to the topic

This lands directly on unfinished business from earlier chapters. Kuhn's account of normal science from Chapter 1 assumed that a paradigm's accumulated findings were basically solid, with the interesting philosophical action happening at the level of anomalies and revolutions — the replication crisis reveals that a meaningful share of "normal science" findings in some fields may never have been solid to begin with, independent of any paradigm-level question. And it sharpens Chapter 3's Duhem-Quine problem in an uncomfortable direction: if a large fraction of positive results are statistical noise dressed up as findings, then a failed replication doesn't just implicate an auxiliary assumption somewhere in the bundle — the "finding" itself may never have reflected a real underlying effect at all, making the whole diagnostic question (what, exactly, went wrong?) even harder to answer with confidence.

The response, and why it counts as the system working

The field's actual response is worth taking seriously as a case study in scientific self-correction, not just a scandal. Pre-registration (publicly committing to a study's hypotheses and planned analysis methods before collecting data, making it much harder to quietly p-hack afterward) has become increasingly standard in psychology and several other fields. Some journals now accept papers based on the quality of a study's design and methods before results are known, specifically to remove the incentive to only submit positive findings. Pre-registered replication attempts, larger sample size requirements, and open data-sharing norms have all spread substantially since 2015. None of this makes the underlying problem disappear retroactively — plenty of pre-2011 findings, in psychology and elsewhere, are still sitting in textbooks without ever having been properly re-tested under the new standards. But the scale and speed of the methodological response is itself a genuine data point in the realism-versus-instrumentalism debate from Chapter 4: whatever else is true about science's relationship to reality, its capacity to notice and correct a systemic error, once measured carefully enough to see it, is real and reasonably fast — which is a meaningfully different claim than saying every individual published finding can be trusted on sight.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Next time you see a single-study finding reported in the news (especially from psychology, nutrition, or social science), ask three questions: was it pre-registered, has it been independently replicated, and what was the sample size — before treating it as settled.
  • Notice your own version of p-hacking — trying multiple explanations for a personal pattern (why a plan failed, why a relationship went a certain way) until one "clicks," then treating that one as the real answer without checking it against the alternatives you discarded.
