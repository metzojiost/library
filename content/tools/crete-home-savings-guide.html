Home Savings Guide — Crete Edition

A working list of DIY and paid home upgrades to cut electricity, water, and heating/cooling costs, sized for a typical Cretan house — hot dry summers driving heavy A/C use, mild wet winters, and some of the best solar exposure in Europe. Ordered roughly cheapest-and-fastest-payback first.

About the numbers: costs and savings below are ballpark ranges based on typical Greek household patterns, assuming electricity around €0.20-0.25/kWh — check your actual bill and adjust. Your real savings depend heavily on your house's size, current insulation, and habits. Treat every figure here as "roughly this," not a quote.

Quick, cheap DIY wins (under €100)

LED bulb swap

€80-120 ~1-1.5 years DIY

Swap incandescent or halogen bulbs for LED equivalents throughout the house. A 40W halogen replaced by a 5W LED, run 3 hours a day, saves roughly €8-9/year per bulb at Greek electricity prices — trivial per bulb, but it adds up fast across 10-15 fixtures, and LEDs last 10-15 years, so most of that decade-plus is pure savings after the first year.

Draft-proofing doors and windows

€30-50 under 1 year DIY

Weatherstripping tape around window frames and a door sweep on exterior doors — a genuinely 30-minute job. Older Cretan homes with aluminum single-pane frames often leak air badly around the edges; sealing those gaps typically cuts heating (and to a lesser extent cooling) losses by 5-10%.

Low-flow showerheads and tap aerators

€20-40 under 1 year DIY

Screw-in fixtures that cut water flow 20-30% with no noticeable pressure loss. Saves on both the water bill and — if your hot water is electric — the energy used heating that water, which matters more than people expect since heating water is one of the bigger line items in a Greek electricity bill.

Smart plug or thermostat timers

€30-80 1-2 years DIY

A cheap smart plug on your water heater (so it's not on 24/7) or a scheduling timer on A/C units prevents the classic "left it running all day" waste. Modest per-device savings, but essentially free to set up once bought.

Reflective window film

€60-150 2-3 years DIY

Self-adhesive film applied to south/west-facing windows that blocks a large share of incoming solar heat before it enters the room — a much cheaper alternative to exterior shutters if full shading isn't in the budget. Noticeably reduces A/C load in rooms that get direct afternoon sun, which in a Cretan summer is most of them.

Medium DIY / moderate cost (€100-1,000)

Rain barrel for garden irrigation

€80-150 1-3 years DIY

A barrel under a downpipe, collecting roof runoff for garden watering — genuinely useful given how dry Cretan summers get and how much a garden can cost to irrigate with mains water. Savings scale with garden size; a small courtyard garden saves less than someone irrigating a real plot.

Ceiling fans (2-3 rooms)

€150-300 2-4 years DIY-able

A ceiling fan lets a room feel comfortable at a higher A/C thermostat setting — each degree you raise the setpoint cuts cooling energy roughly 7-10%. Running a fan alongside A/C rather than A/C alone at max is one of the highest comfort-per-euro moves available. Installation is DIY-able if there's already wiring for a ceiling light; otherwise budget for an electrician.

Cool/reflective roof coating

€400-600 4-8 years DIY-able

Flat concrete roofs are standard across Greece, and they absorb an enormous amount of summer heat that radiates straight down into the top floor. A white or reflective elastomeric coating, rolled on like paint, cuts that heat gain substantially — noticeably cooler top-floor rooms and real A/C savings, plus it extends the roof's waterproofing life as a bonus. Doable as a weekend DIY project on a modest roof; steep or large roofs are worth paying for.

Bigger investments, usually worth a professional (€1,000+)

Solar water heater (ηλιακός θερμοσίφωνας)

€700-1,200 3-5 years Professional

The single best-value big upgrade for a Cretan home, and it's everywhere here for good reason — the solar exposure genuinely makes it work. A 200L system typically covers 70-90% of a household's hot water needs for most of the year with essentially no running cost, only backing off in the depths of winter. If your hot water is currently electric, this is usually the highest-value single purchase on this whole list.

Replacing an old A/C with a modern inverter unit

€700-1,200 per unit 4-6 years Professional

A non-inverter unit older than about 10 years can use 30-50% more electricity than a modern A+++ inverter model to deliver the same cooling. Given how many hours A/C actually runs in a Cretan summer, this adds up. Worth prioritizing for whichever unit gets the heaviest daily use, rather than replacing everything at once.

Exterior shutters or awnings

€750-1,500 10-20 years Professional

Exterior shading blocks solar heat before it hits the glass at all, which is far more effective than interior blinds or curtains — but the payback here is genuinely long on energy savings alone. The honest case for this one is comfort and property value as much as pure savings; if budget is tight, the reflective window film above gets you a meaningful share of the benefit for a fraction of the cost.

Heat pump water heater

€1,200-2,000 6-10 years Professional

Uses a heat pump instead of resistance elements to heat water — 3-4x more efficient than a standard electric boiler. Makes the most sense as a backup/booster paired with a solar water heater for winter and cloudy days, or as the main system if a full solar installation isn't practical for your roof.

Double-glazed windows (whole house)

€3,000-6,000 15-30 years unsubsidized Professional

Meaningfully reduces both heating and cooling load, but the honest math is that new windows alone rarely pay for themselves on energy savings within a reasonable timeframe — unless your current windows are genuinely bad (rusted single-pane aluminum with visible gaps) or you access a subsidy. This is the item on the list most worth applying for Εξοικονομώ funding before committing to.

Wall/roof insulation retrofit (external thermal façade)

€6,000-12,000 15-25 years unsubsidized Professional

Uninsulated poured-concrete walls — standard in older Greek construction — are a genuine thermal liability, losing heat fast in winter and gaining it fast in summer. This is the highest-impact structural upgrade available (potentially 30-50% off combined heating/cooling costs) but also the most expensive, and like windows, the standalone payback is long. Same advice: this is a subsidy-program project, not a pay-full-price one.

Solar PV panels with net metering

€5,000-8,000 6-9 years Professional

A roughly 4kWp system can offset most of an average household's electricity use under Greece's net-metering scheme, where surplus daytime production banks against what you draw at night. After payback, you're looking at 15+ more years of panel life at near-zero electricity cost — panels are typically warrantied 20-25 years. The biggest upfront number on this list, but also the biggest long-run win if you can afford it.

Check this before paying full price for anything big

The Εξοικονομώ subsidy program

Greece periodically runs "Εξοικονομώ" ("Exoikonomo," meaning "I save"), a government energy-efficiency subsidy program that co-funds insulation, window replacement, solar water heaters, A/C upgrades, and sometimes solar PV — the subsidy percentage depends on household income and typically ranges from roughly a third to well over half the cost, historically higher for lower-income applicants. Programs run in funding cycles that open and close, and exact terms, eligible categories, and subsidy rates change between cycles, so treat any specific number you've heard as out of date until you check the current cycle yourself via gov.gr or a local energy consultant ("ενεργειακός επιθεωρητής"). For the two biggest-ticket items on this list — windows and wall insulation — this program is often the difference between a 20-year payback and a genuinely reasonable one, so it's worth checking before committing to either.

If you only do three things from this list: a solar water heater, LED bulbs plus draft-proofing, and a ceiling fan or two cover most of the easy wins — cheap, fast payback, and they address the two biggest costs in a Cretan home (hot water and summer cooling) directly.
