Chapter 10: One Decision, Run Through All Nine Tools

~3 min read

Nine chapters have built up nine distinct tools: opportunity cost (Chapter 1), the marginal decision rule (Chapter 2), breakeven analysis (Chapter 3), diminishing returns (Chapter 4), economies of scale (Chapter 5), the sunk-cost rule (Chapter 6), the boundary of a firm (Chapter 7), time's full price (Chapter 8), and marginal-thinking budgeting (Chapter 9). This closing chapter is a single worked example, running one real decision through the full set — the practical proof that these aren't nine separate ideas, but one coherent way of thinking about any resource-allocation problem, whatever it's about.

The decision: should you take on a side project?

Someone is offered a recurring freelance project: $400 a month, roughly 8 hours of work. Here's the full nine-step pass.

Running it through all nine tools

1. Opportunity cost: what would those 8 monthly hours otherwise produce — rest, a different paid opportunity, time on an existing higher-leverage project? Name the real alternative, not a vague "free time."

2. Marginal analysis: the $400 is the marginal benefit; the real marginal cost is those 8 hours' opportunity cost plus any actual expenses (software, materials) — not compared to an average hourly rate across all existing work, but to this specific block of time's next-best use.

3. Breakeven: if the project requires any upfront investment (new software, a course to learn a needed skill), how many months of the $400 payment does it take to break even on that investment before it's genuinely profitable?

4. Diminishing returns: is this 8th, 9th, 10th hour of work each week landing on already-scarce attention and energy, where the actual output quality per hour is lower than it would be on a fresher day?

5. Economies of scale: could this specific project, or a few like it, be batched or streamlined in a way that lowers the effective time cost per project as more of them are taken on?

6. Sunk costs: if this decision follows an earlier failed attempt at a similar project, is that earlier failure being allowed to influence this decision at all — and should it be (the stranger test from Chapter 6)?

7. Transaction costs / firm boundary: is this a one-off, or does it recur — and past what recurrence point would it make more sense to build a repeatable system or process for it, rather than treating each instance as a fresh negotiation?

8. Time's full price: what is that 8 hours honestly worth, at its real opportunity cost — not "free time" by default, and not necessarily the person's normal hourly rate either, but its actual best alternative use this specific month?

9. Budget fit: re-justified from zero (Chapter 9), does taking this on actually displace something more valuable already in the schedule, or genuinely fill previously unallocated capacity?

None of these nine questions require knowing anything about a market, a competitor, or a price trend — which was the point of building this topic entirely around the inward-facing half of economics. The tools are simple individually. What makes them powerful is running the full set deliberately, in writing, rather than defaulting to whichever one or two happen to come to mind first.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Take one real decision currently in front of you — work, business, or personal — and actually run all nine questions on it, in writing, the same way the side-project example above did. The value is in seeing all nine answers laid out together, not in any single one alone.
