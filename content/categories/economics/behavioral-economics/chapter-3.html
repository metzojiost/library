Chapter 1 introduced anchoring: an irrelevant number, once seen, quietly pulls a later estimate toward itself. That was one specific example of a much bigger pattern. When people judge how likely, how common, or how probable something is, they very rarely calculate — they substitute a faster, related question that feels like it should give the same answer, and usually doesn't.

Availability: vivid examples feel more common than they are

The availability heuristic (heuristic = a mental shortcut — fast, but not always accurate) is the tendency to judge frequency by how easily examples come to mind, rather than by actual statistics. Vivid, emotionally striking, heavily reported events (plane crashes, shark attacks, dramatic financial crashes) get overestimated in probability, while mundane, common, poorly reported risks (heart disease, car accidents, slow financial decay from fees) get underestimated — not from ignorance, but because the vivid cases are simply easier to retrieve from memory, and ease of retrieval gets mistaken for actual frequency.

Representativeness: judging by resemblance to a stereotype

A second, related shortcut is the representativeness heuristic: judging the probability of something by how much it resembles a familiar category or prototype, while ignoring how statistically common that category actually is. Kahneman and Tversky demonstrated this with a now-famous example, sometimes called the "Linda problem." Participants read a description of "Linda" — outspoken, philosophy major, deeply concerned with social justice — then were asked which is more probable: "Linda is a bank teller," or "Linda is a bank teller and active in the feminist movement." A large majority chose the second option, because it fits the vivid character sketch better. But this is a basic logical error: the odds of two things both being true (bank teller and feminist) can never be higher than the odds of just one of them being true (bank teller alone) — every feminist bank teller is also, trivially, a bank teller. This specific error, choosing the more "representative" but logically impossible option, is called the conjunction fallacy.

Base-rate neglect: ignoring how common something actually is

A closely related failure is base-rate neglect — ignoring the underlying background frequency of something in favor of specific, vivid evidence in front of you. A well-known medical version: imagine a disease that affects 1 in 1,000 people, and a test for it that's 95% accurate. If you test positive, what's the actual chance you have the disease? Most people's gut answer is "around 95%." The real answer, once you account for the base rate, is closer to 2% — because the test's false positives, applied across the huge pool of healthy people, vastly outnumber its correct positives among the tiny pool of actually sick people. The test result feels like the whole story; the base rate quietly does most of the actual work, and gets ignored anyway.

The planning fallacy: your own past doesn't seem to teach you

These same shortcuts combine to produce the planning fallacy: the well-documented tendency to underestimate how long a task will take or how much it will cost, even for people who have personally blown past similar estimates before, repeatedly. Anchoring plays a role (people anchor on the best-case timeline first, then adjust too little); availability plays a role too (it's easier to vividly imagine things going smoothly than to recall the specific ways past projects went wrong). The result shows up everywhere from home renovations to national infrastructure projects, which reliably run over budget and over schedule by predictable margins, even when the people involved know, in the abstract, that this always happens.

What ties all four of these together, and back to Chapter 1's anchoring, is the same underlying story: judging probability accurately is hard and slow, so the mind quietly substitutes an easier question — how vivid is this? how well does it match a stereotype? how specific is this scenario? — and answers that instead, with total confidence that it answered the original question.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Try the planning fallacy on your next estimate: before starting a task, write down your time estimate, then deliberately add 50% before committing to a deadline — notice how uncomfortable that feels, which is itself a sign of how strong the bias is.
  • Notice base-rate neglect in health or news stories: next time you see a scary statistic about a test or a risk, ask what the actual underlying base rate is before reacting to the headline number alone.
