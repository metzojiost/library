Chapter 1 closed with a genuine puzzle: "open-label" placebos, where patients are told honestly and explicitly that the pill they're taking contains no active medication, still produce measurable effects in multiple well-controlled trials. This seems to directly contradict the intuitive assumption that placebo effects require some degree of deception — that the whole mechanism depends on the patient not knowing. It doesn't, and working out why is one of the more genuinely surprising findings in the field.

The trial that made researchers take this seriously

Physician and researcher Ted Kaptchuk, at Harvard Medical School, ran a landmark 2010 trial on patients with irritable bowel syndrome, randomly assigning one group to receive no treatment and another group to receive placebo pills — dispensed in bottles explicitly labeled "placebo pills," with patients told plainly, in writing, that the pills contained no medication and worked only through the body's own self-healing processes activated by the act of taking a pill. The open-label placebo group reported significantly greater symptom relief than the no-treatment group, and by a margin comparable to what's typically seen for actual IBS medications in other trials. Kaptchuk and colleagues have since replicated this basic finding across a range of conditions, including chronic lower back pain, cancer-related fatigue, and migraine, with the open-label placebo consistently outperforming no treatment at all.

Why honesty doesn't kill the effect

The leading explanation reframes what a placebo pill actually is doing, and it follows directly from Chapter 1's expectation-pathway evidence: if placebo effects work partly through classical conditioning — the ritual of taking a pill, on a schedule, engaging with a caregiver, has itself been paired, over a lifetime of real medical experience, with genuine relief — then the ritual itself can trigger part of the physiological response, independent of whether the patient consciously believes the specific pill in front of them is "real." Kaptchuk's own interpretation emphasizes the broader clinical ritual — being cared for, engaging in a structured healing behavior, the symbolic act of taking medicine — as doing meaningful work on its own, separate from the deceptive belief that the pill itself contains an active drug.

It isn't effortless, and the framing given still matters

Open-label placebo isn't simply "any sugar pill, told to anyone, works the same regardless of framing." Trials that saw the strongest effects delivered the open-label pills alongside a persuasive, confident rationale — explaining the placebo effect's real physiological basis to patients, similar to the framing Chapter 1 covered, rather than simply handing over a labeled bottle with no further context. This suggests open-label placebo isn't purely about the absence of deception, but also about a different kind of honest persuasion — convincing a patient that a real, evidenced physiological mechanism is available to them, which happens to be true, rather than convincing them a fake drug is a real one, which is not.

Why this genuinely matters for medical ethics

The clinical and ethical implications are significant, and this thread will be picked up directly in this topic's final chapter: if placebo effects can be harnessed without deceiving the patient, a major ethical objection to using placebo therapeutically — that it necessarily requires lying to patients, which conflicts with informed consent — no longer applies in the same way. Several researchers, Kaptchuk included, have proposed open-label placebo as a legitimate, low-cost, low-side-effect complement to standard treatment for certain chronic, subjective-symptom conditions, precisely because it sidesteps the deception problem that has made placebo use in real clinical practice ethically fraught for decades.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Notice the ritual, not just the pill: next time you take any medication, pay attention to how much of the experience — the routine, the care, the act itself — might be doing work independent of the drug's pharmacology.
  • Consider Kaptchuk's framing choice: would you find "this pill has no active ingredient, but your body's own healing response to the ritual of taking it is real and measurable" a persuasive, honest pitch? Most trial participants did.
