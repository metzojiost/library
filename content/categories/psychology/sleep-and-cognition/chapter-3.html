Chapter 2 covered why falling asleep can be hard for some people. This chapter covers a related but separate question: once asleep, why do some people stay asleep through a phone buzzing across the room, while others snap awake at a floorboard creak two rooms away?

Sleep spindles: the brain's noise filter

During light and deep non-REM sleep, the brain periodically generates brief bursts of fast, rhythmic electrical activity called sleep spindles. Their leading function, well-supported by sleep lab research, is protective: spindles appear to actively suppress the brain's response to external sensory input, essentially gating incoming sound and light information before it can trigger a full waking response. Researcher Jeffrey Ellenbogen and others have shown, using overnight sound tests, that people who naturally produce more sleep spindles per night sleep through disruptive noise significantly more often than people who produce fewer — a real, individually variable trait, not something under conscious control.

K-complexes: the brain's other response to a disturbance

A related structure, the K-complex, is a single large, sharp wave that appears in response to a stimulus during non-REM sleep — sometimes followed by a spindle, sometimes not. K-complexes seem to serve a dual, almost contradictory role: registering that something happened (a kind of brief, low-level "noted") while simultaneously helping to suppress a full cortical awakening in response to it. In effect, they let the sleeping brain acknowledge a disturbance without necessarily acting on it — evidence that "sleeping through something" isn't the same as the brain not registering it at all; a surprising amount of processing continues even in stimuli that never reach conscious awareness the next morning.

Arousal threshold changes across the night, and across sleep stages

The ease of waking isn't fixed across a single night either — it varies predictably with sleep stage, tying directly back to Chapter 1's architecture. Arousal threshold is highest (hardest to wake) during deep slow-wave sleep, concentrated earlier in the night, and lowest (easiest to wake) during REM sleep and light sleep, more concentrated later in the night. Someone who wakes easily, especially later in the night or from what feels like a light, fragile stage, isn't imagining a difference — they're very plausibly waking from stages where the brain's own gating mechanisms are naturally weaker to begin with, compounding with any individual difference in spindle density on top of that.

Not necessarily a disorder — often just a trait

It's worth being precise here: a lower arousal threshold, on its own, isn't a sleep disorder — plenty of light, easily-woken sleepers have no diagnosable condition at all, and some research frames a lower threshold as a plausible evolutionary trait (staying more responsive to environmental threats) rather than a malfunction. It becomes a practical problem specifically when the environment is genuinely noisy or disruptive and there's no way to reduce that input — which is a fixable engineering problem (Chapter 5 covers this directly), not necessarily evidence that something is wrong with the sleeper.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Notice when in the night you tend to wake: if it's consistently later in the night rather than early, that lines up with lighter, more easily disrupted sleep stages being more common later on — not a random or concerning pattern.
  • Try one environmental change for a week — earplugs, a white noise machine, or blackout curtains, whichever matches what actually wakes you most (sound, light, or a mix) — and track whether your number of awakenings changes.
