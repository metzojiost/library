Polish Language Guide

A beginner's first ~20 hours with Polish — pronunciation, the core grammar that makes Polish famously tricky (and how to survive it), everyday vocabulary, and enough real sentence patterns to actually function. Polish is a genuinely hard language for English speakers, mostly because of its case system — this guide doesn't hide that, it just breaks it into pieces small enough to actually stick.

This is the static, read-through version. The same curriculum also exists as an interactive tool — chunked drilling, multiple-choice/typed/matching quizzes, and spaced-repetition review — if you want to compare the two formats directly.

A. Foundations

Hour 1: The Alphabet & Pronunciation

~60 minutes

Polish uses the Latin alphabet plus nine extra letters, and — unlike English — its spelling is almost completely regular: once you learn the rules, you can pronounce nearly any Polish word correctly on sight. There's no q, v, or x (they only show up in foreign names). The good news up front: stress is almost always on the second-to-last syllable, with very few exceptions. You basically never have to guess.

Letter/digraphSounds likeExample
c"ts" as in catsco (what)
cz"ch" as in church (hard)czas (time)
ć / cisoft "ch", like cheese but gentlerćwiczenie (exercise)
sz"sh" as in shopszkoła (school)
ś / sisoft "sh"proszę (please)
rz / żlike the "s" in pleasurerzeka (river), żona (wife)
ź / zisoft version of rz/żźle (badly)
dz"ds" as in ladsdzwon (bell)
dź / dzisoft "j" as in jeansdziękuję (thank you)
dżhard "j" as in jungledżem (jam)
ch / hsame sound — like Scottish "loch"chleb (bread), herbata (tea)
łEnglish "w"ładny (pretty) — sounds like "wadny"
wEnglish "v"woda (water) — sounds like "voda"

Notice the pattern: a letter with an accent (ć, ś, ź, ń) is the "soft" version of its plain counterpart, and the two-letter versions (ci, si, zi, ni) are the same soft sound written differently depending on what follows. You don't need to memorize the rule for which spelling to use yet — just recognize both forms sound the same.

How would you pronounce "Warszawa" (the Polish name for Warsaw)?var-SHA-va — sz = "sh", w = "v", stress on the second-to-last syllable.
What does "ł" sound like, and what does that make "Łódź" (a Polish city) sound like?"ł" = English "w". Łódź sounds roughly like "wooj" (the ó = "oo", dź = soft "j").

Hour 2: Greetings & Politeness

~60 minutes

Polish has a formality distinction English lost centuries ago: you address strangers, elders, and anyone you're not close with using pan (sir/Mr.) or pani (madam/Mrs./Ms.) instead of "you" — roughly like the French vous or Spanish usted. Getting this right matters more in Polish social etiquette than most beginners expect.

PolishPronunciationEnglish
CześćcheshchHi / Bye (informal)
Dzień dobryjen DOH-briGood day (formal hello, any time before evening)
Dobry wieczórDOH-bri VYE-choorGood evening
Do widzeniadoh veed-ZEN-yaGoodbye (formal)
ProszęPROH-shehPlease / here you go / you're welcome
Dziękujęjen-KOO-yehThank you
Przepraszampsheh-PRAH-shamExcuse me / I'm sorry
Tak / Nietahk / nyehYes / No
Pan / Panipahn / PAH-neeSir / Madam (formal "you")
Miło miMEE-woh meeNice to meet you
Dzień dobry, jak się pan/pani ma? — Good day, how are you? (formal, to a man/woman)
Cześć, jak się masz? — Hi, how are you? (informal)
Dziękuję, dobrze. A ty? — Thanks, good. And you?
Honest heads-up: Polish formality isn't just "pan/pani vs. you" — the whole sentence often restructures around it (verbs go into third person, as if talking about someone rather than to them: "jak się pani ma?" literally "how does the madam have herself?"). You don't need to master this yet — just recognize it and default to formal with strangers.

Hour 4: Pronouns, "To Be" & "To Have"

~60 minutes

Two verbs to learn cold before anything else, because they're everywhere: być (to be) and mieć (to have). One genuinely convenient Polish feature: subject pronouns (ja, ty, on...) are usually dropped, because the verb ending already tells you who's doing the action — much like Spanish or Italian.

Pronounbyć (to be)mieć (to have)
ja (I)jestemmam
ty (you, sg.)jesteśmasz
on/ona/ono (he/she/it)jestma
my (we)jesteśmymamy
wy (you, pl.)jesteściemacie
oni/one (they)sąmają
Jestem zmęczony. — I am tired. (a man speaking — note the -y ending, more on adjective gender in Hour 12)
Mam psa. — I have a dog.
Czy masz czas? — Do you have time? ("czy" flags a yes/no question — see Hour 11)

B. Grammar Core

Hour 5: Noun Gender

~60 minutes

Every Polish noun has a gender — masculine, feminine, or neuter — and gender affects the endings of adjectives, past-tense verbs, and more, so it's worth building the instinct early. The good news: gender is usually predictable from the noun's ending.

GenderUsually ends inExamples
Masculinea consonantdom (house), pies (dog), stół (table)
Feminine-a (mostly)kobieta (woman), szkoła (school), książka (book)
Neuter-o, -e, or -ędziecko (child), mieszkanie (apartment), imię (first name)

Exceptions exist (a handful of masculine nouns end in -a, like "mężczyzna" — man — because they refer to male people), but the pattern above correctly predicts gender for the large majority of nouns you'll meet early on.

What gender is "kawa" (coffee), and how do you know?Feminine — it ends in -a.
What gender is "okno" (window)?Neuter — it ends in -o.

Hour 6: The Case System — Why It Exists

~60 minutes

This is the hour most guides skip past too quickly, and it's the single biggest reason Polish has a reputation for being hard. Polish nouns, pronouns, and adjectives change their ending depending on their grammatical role in the sentence — a system called cases. English does a faint version of this with pronouns ("I" vs "me" vs "my"), but Polish does it to every noun, all the time, across seven cases.

CaseRough jobExample question it answers
Mianownik (Nominative)the subject — who/what is doing the actionWho? What?
Dopełniacz (Genitive)possession, negation, "of"Whose? Of what?
Celownik (Dative)the indirect object — who something is given/said toTo whom?
Biernik (Accusative)the direct object — who/what receives the actionWhom? What?
Narzędnik (Instrumental)"by means of," "with"With what/whom?
Miejscownik (Locative)location, always after certain prepositionsWhere?
Wołacz (Vocative)directly addressing someone(calling out to someone)
Honest heads-up: you will not master all seven cases in 20 hours, and no beginner guide that promises that is being straight with you. What you can do in this time is get genuinely comfortable with the three most useful ones — Nominative, Accusative, and Genitive — which cover a huge share of everyday sentences. The next two hours focus entirely on those three; the rest can wait.

Hour 7: Nominative & Accusative in Practice

~75 minutes

Nominative is the "dictionary form" — the form you'll find in a phrasebook, and the one used for the subject of a sentence. Accusative is used for the direct object (the thing receiving the action) and is one of the more frequently used cases day to day, e.g. after verbs like "mieć" (to have), "lubić" (to like), "widzieć" (to see).

The good news: for most feminine and neuter nouns, and virtually all masculine inanimate nouns, the accusative form is identical to the nominative. The one place it reliably changes is feminine nouns ending in -a, where -a becomes -ę, and masculine animate nouns (people, animals), which take a different accusative ending.

NominativeAccusativeExample sentence
książka (book, fem.)książkęCzytam książkę. (I'm reading a book.)
kawa (coffee, fem.)kawęLubię kawę. (I like coffee.)
dom (house, masc. inanimate)dom (unchanged)Mam dom. (I have a house.)
pies (dog, masc. animate)psaMam psa. (I have a dog.)
okno (window, neuter)okno (unchanged)Widzę okno. (I see a window.)
How would you say "I like tea" — herbata (tea) is feminine, ending in -a?Lubię herbatę — the -a of herbata becomes -ę in the accusative.

Hour 8: Genitive Case & Negation

~75 minutes

The genitive case is worth its own hour because of one rule that trips up almost every English-speaking learner: Polish negation switches the object into the genitive case, not the accusative you'd expect. "I have a dog" uses accusative (Mam psa), but "I don't have a dog" switches to genitive (Nie mam psa — here it happens to look the same, but with feminine nouns the difference is obvious).

NominativeGenitiveExample
kawa (coffee)kawyNie mam kawy. (I don't have coffee.)
czas (time)czasuNie mam czasu. (I don't have time.)
pieniądze (money)pieniędzyNie mam pieniędzy. (I don't have money.)

Genitive also handles possession (the equivalent of English "'s" or "of"), and is required after common prepositions like bez (without), dla (for), and do (to/toward).

dom mojej siostry — my sister's house (literally "house of my sister")
Idę do sklepu. — I'm going to the store. (sklep → sklepu)
Kawa bez cukru. — Coffee without sugar. (cukier → cukru)

C. Verbs & Sentences

Hour 9: Present Tense Conjugation Patterns

~60 minutes

Most Polish verbs fall into a handful of predictable conjugation patterns. Learning these three covers the large majority of verbs you'll use as a beginner.

Pronounczytać (to read)mówić (to speak)pracować (to work)

Hour 11: Asking Questions

~60 minutes

PolishEnglish

Gdzie jest dworzec? — Where is the train station?

Ile to kosztuje? — How much does this cost?

"Czy" has no direct English equivalent — it just flags "a yes/no question is coming," similar to the upside-down question mark that opens a question in Spanish. Word order in Polish is also much more flexible than English, so "czy" is often the clearest signal that a question, not a statement, is being made.

Hour 12: Adjectives & Agreement

~60 minutes

Polish adjectives change their ending to match the gender of the noun they describe — a pattern called agreement.

GenderTypical endingExample
Masculine-y (or -i)ładny dom (a nice house)
Feminine-aładna kobieta (a nice woman)
Neuter-eładne dziecko (a nice child)

This also applies to describing yourself: a man says "jestem zmęczony" (I'm tired), a woman says "jestem zmęczona" — same word, different ending, matching the speaker's own gender.

Hour 17: Past Tense (and Why It's Gendered)

~75 minutes

Here's a genuinely distinctive Polish feature: the past tense agrees with the speaker's own gender, not just the grammatical gender of a noun. Formed from the infinitive stem plus -ł- and a personal ending.

onrobiłhe did
onarobiłashe did

Hour 18: Future Tense & a Warning About Aspect

~60 minutes

For everyday plans, the simplest future is będę + infinitive: "będę czytać" (I will read), "będę pracować" (I will work) — this pattern works for any verb of this type and is enough to get you through most beginner conversations about the future.

Będę czytać książkę. — I will read a book.
Jutro będziemy pracować. — Tomorrow we will work.
Honest heads-up — this is the biggest thing left out of this guide on purpose: Polish verbs come in pairs called aspect — an "imperfective" version (ongoing/repeated actions, like czytać) and a "perfective" version (a single completed action, like przeczytać) — and which one you use changes the meaning in ways English simply doesn't mark. Mastering aspect is widely considered the hardest part of Polish grammar, genuinely takes years, and can't be meaningfully compressed into a beginner guide. The będę + infinitive pattern above will get you through 20 hours' worth of conversations just fine — treat aspect as the next real mountain once this guide is done, not something to worry about yet.
