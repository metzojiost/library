Chapter 1 flagged the debate briefly: does personality genuinely require a sixth dimension, or does the original five-factor model already cover everything statistically justified? Psychologists Kibeom Lee and Michael Ashton built the most serious, sustained challenge to the five-factor consensus using the exact same empirical method the Big Five itself was built on — which is what makes their case worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as an arbitrary addition.

Using the Big Five's own method against its own conclusion

Recall from Chapter 1 that the Big Five wasn't derived from a theorist's armchair framework — it emerged from factor analysis applied to how people describe personality in ordinary language across large datasets. Lee and Ashton, running comparable lexical studies across several additional languages beyond the English-heavy corpus much of the original Big Five research drew on, found that a six-factor solution consistently fit the data at least as well as five, and that the sixth factor — which didn't map cleanly onto any of the original five — kept appearing with strikingly similar content across culturally and linguistically diverse language samples: a cluster of traits related to sincerity, fairness, greed-avoidance, and modesty. They named this sixth dimension Honesty-Humility, and their full model — HEXACO, an acronym for Honesty-Humility, Emotionality, eXtraversion, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, and Openness — restructures the original five slightly (Emotionality and Agreeableness shift some content between them compared to the Big Five's Neuroticism and Agreeableness) around this added sixth axis.

What Honesty-Humility actually measures

The Honesty-Humility dimension spans a spectrum from sincere, fair, modest, and genuinely uninterested in status or unearned advantage, at one end, to manipulative, entitled, self-important, and willing to exploit others for personal gain, at the other. This is conceptually distinct from Big Five Agreeableness, which centers more on warmth, cooperativeness, and tolerance in ongoing interpersonal interaction — a person can score low on Honesty-Humility (willing to exploit others when it benefits them and they can get away with it) while still scoring reasonably high on Agreeableness in everyday interaction (pleasant, cooperative, easy to get along with day to day), a combination the original five-factor model has real difficulty capturing cleanly, since both traits get partially blended into Agreeableness's single dimension when Honesty-Humility isn't measured separately.

Why this dimension turned out to matter for predicting real behavior

Honesty-Humility's practical case rests substantially on what it predicts that the original Big Five predicts more weakly. Research consistently finds low Honesty-Humility scores correlate with the Dark Triad (a cluster of three related, socially aversive personality traits studied together: narcissism — grandiosity and entitlement; Machiavellianism — strategic manipulation and cynicism about others' motives; and psychopathy — low empathy and impulsive antisocial behavior) more strongly and more consistently than any single Big Five dimension does on its own, including Agreeableness. Low Honesty-Humility also shows meaningful predictive power for counterproductive workplace behavior — theft, exploitation of colleagues, unethical shortcuts for personal advantage — sometimes outperforming Big Five Agreeableness and Conscientiousness combined in predicting exactly these outcomes in real organizational studies. This gives HEXACO's added dimension a genuine practical case beyond statistical tidiness: it captures a specific, consequential behavioral pattern (willingness to exploit others for personal gain) that the original five-factor structure spreads thin across other dimensions and predicts less precisely as a result.

Why the field hasn't fully settled on six

Despite this genuine evidence, the Big Five remains the more widely used model in most research and applied settings, for reasons that are more about momentum and infrastructure than about HEXACO being wrong. Decades of existing research, validated assessment instruments, and normative data are built around the five-factor structure, and switching a field's standard measurement tool carries real transition costs independent of which model is more accurate. Some researchers also argue the practical gain from adding a sixth dimension is real but modest for many research purposes, while being more substantial specifically for predicting the antisocial, exploitative behaviors Honesty-Humility targets most directly — meaning the right choice between the two models depends considerably on what a given study or application is actually trying to predict, rather than one model being straightforwardly superior across the board.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Think of someone you'd describe as agreeable in everyday interaction but who you'd also hesitate to fully trust with something valuable — that gap is close to what HEXACO's separation of Agreeableness from Honesty-Humility is trying to capture.
  • Notice the next time you evaluate someone's trustworthiness specifically, rather than their general pleasantness — see if you're actually tracking something closer to Honesty-Humility than to Big Five Agreeableness.
