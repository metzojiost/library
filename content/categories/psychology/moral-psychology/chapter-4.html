Chapter 3 ended on a pointed caveat: essentially everything covered in this topic so far — the six moral foundations, moral dumbfounding, the trolley and footbridge studies — was built and tested overwhelmingly on Western, and specifically American and European, research participants. That matters more here than in many areas of psychology, because morality is precisely the kind of domain where culture might be expected to run deep, and where the research base's actual scope deserves a direct, honest look.

Individualizing versus binding foundations, and who weights which

Chapter 1 already noted a political split within Western samples: politically liberal respondents weight care and fairness heavily and the other four foundations lightly, while politically conservative respondents draw more evenly across all six. Haidt and colleagues group care and fairness together as individualizing foundations (moral concerns centered on protecting individuals from harm and ensuring fair treatment between individuals), and loyalty, authority, and sanctity as binding foundations (moral concerns centered on maintaining group cohesion, hierarchy, and collective purity — binding individuals into a larger social unit). Cross-cultural surveys extending this framework beyond Western liberal democracies have found that reliance on binding foundations is, if anything, the more common global pattern historically and cross-culturally — many non-Western and non-secular societies weight loyalty, authority, and sanctity considerably more heavily, and more uniformly across the political spectrum, than the individualizing-heavy profile that dominates secular Western liberal samples. This flips a framing that can creep in unexamined: the individualizing-foundations-only profile isn't a neutral, universal moral baseline that other cultures deviate from — it's itself a comparatively unusual profile, concentrated in a specific, historically recent cultural context.

Disgust and sanctity: culturally calibrated, not fixed

Chapter 1's hook about disgust predicting moral judgment gets more interesting, and more variable, once you look across cultures: what specifically triggers moral disgust — which foods, which bodily practices, which social arrangements — differs substantially by culture, even while the underlying disgust-to-morality link (something feels physically repellent, therefore something feels morally wrong) appears to operate broadly across very different societies. This is a genuinely useful pattern to hold onto: the mechanism linking a bodily reaction to a moral verdict looks close to universal; the specific triggers that activate it are learned, culturally transmitted, and can vary enormously — meaning two people from different cultural backgrounds can have the identical underlying moral-psychological machinery and still reach opposite verdicts on the same practice, because their sanctity foundation was calibrated on different specific content growing up.

The expanding moral circle — a genuine historical pattern, worth separating from the foundations debate

A related but distinct line of thinking, developed by philosopher Peter Singer under the term the expanding circle, tracks something more like a historical direction than a cross-sectional cultural variation: across recorded history, the boundary of who counts as a legitimate object of moral concern — Chapter 1's care foundation, specifically — has tended, on average and with real setbacks and reversals along the way, to widen: from immediate family and tribe, to co-religionists, to fellow citizens, to all of humanity, and for a growing number of people today, to animals capable of suffering. This isn't a claim that any specific culture's foundations are more or less correct — it's a separate, historical observation about which group of beings a given moral community has tended to include inside its zone of active moral concern, and it offers a useful complementary lens to the foundations framework: not just which foundations a culture weights, but how widely each foundation's protections actually extend.

What holding both truths together looks like

The honest synthesis here mirrors a pattern this library keeps returning to: a genuinely universal psychological mechanism (six foundations, an intuition-first judgment process, a disgust-morality link) operating on genuinely variable cultural content (which foundations get weighted heavily, what specifically triggers disgust, how wide the circle of concern currently extends). Neither half of that sentence cancels the other out. Recognizing the mechanism as broadly universal doesn't mean assuming any one culture's specific moral profile is the neutral default everyone else deviates from — and recognizing real cultural variation doesn't mean morality is purely arbitrary or that no moral view can be evaluated as more or less consistent, more or less widely applied, or more or less willing to extend its own stated principles evenly.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Notice a moral reaction of disgust you have toward an unfamiliar practice from another culture — and ask whether you're evaluating actual harm, or a sanctity-foundation reaction calibrated on your own specific upbringing.
  • Trace your own "circle" of moral concern: who and what genuinely falls inside it for you today, and try to identify one boundary that's shifted, even slightly, over your own lifetime.
