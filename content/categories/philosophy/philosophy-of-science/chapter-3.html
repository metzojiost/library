Chapter 1's falsificationism made testing sound clean: propose a bold prediction, run the experiment, and either the theory survives or it's disproven. Chapter 2 already complicated that picture with underdetermination. This chapter covers an even more direct problem with the clean version of falsification — one that shows a "failed" test almost never actually points a finger cleanly at the theory being tested.

You never test one hypothesis alone

Physicist and philosopher Pierre Duhem first laid this out around 1906, and philosopher W.V.O. Quine extended it decades later, so it's now known as the Duhem-Quine problem (the observation that a scientific hypothesis can never be tested in complete isolation — any real experiment also depends on a whole bundle of auxiliary assumptions, and a failed prediction only shows that something in the whole bundle is wrong, not which part). Consider what it actually takes to test even a simple physics hypothesis: you need the hypothesis itself, plus assumptions about how your measuring instruments work, plus background theories about the conditions of the experiment, plus assumptions that nothing unaccounted-for is interfering. When the predicted result doesn't show up, logic alone can't tell you whether the core hypothesis is false, or whether the instrument was miscalibrated, or whether a background assumption was wrong, or whether some unmodeled disturbance crept in. A single failed prediction implicates the entire web of assumptions simultaneously — not the hypothesis being tested in isolation, because no such isolated test actually exists.

A real case where the "failure" pointed the other way entirely

In the 19th century, astronomers observed that the planet Uranus's actual orbit didn't match what Newtonian mechanics predicted — a clear, textbook falsifying result for Newton's theory of gravity, by the strict logic of Chapter 1. But rather than concluding Newton's laws were false, astronomers Urbain Le Verrier and John Couch Adams independently suspected the auxiliary assumption — that the solar system contained only the planets already known — was the actual problem. Le Verrier calculated where an additional, undiscovered planet would need to be to produce exactly Uranus's observed deviations, and in 1846, astronomer Johann Galle pointed a telescope at that exact location and found Neptune. The "falsifying" observation hadn't refuted Newtonian mechanics at all — it had refuted an unstated background assumption about the solar system's contents, and Newton's theory came out of the episode stronger than before.

The same structure, used the other way, to protect a bad theory

The Neptune case shows the Duhem-Quine problem working productively — the ambiguity got resolved by good further science. But the identical logical structure can also be used defensively, to shield a genuinely bad theory from ever being falsified: when a prediction fails, simply blame an auxiliary assumption every time, patch it with an ad hoc modification (a change made solely to save a theory from a specific failed prediction, with no independent justification or new predictive power of its own), and the core theory never has to face a real test. This is precisely the failure mode Popper's falsificationism was designed to rule out as unscientific — and the Duhem-Quine problem shows why ruling it out isn't as simple as Popper's original framework suggested, since distinguishing a legitimate auxiliary-assumption fix (like discovering Neptune) from an illegitimate, evidence-dodging patch isn't something pure logic can settle on its own. The usual, imperfect answer echoes Chapter 2's escape from underdetermination: a legitimate fix should be independently testable and should predict genuinely new things (Neptune was later directly observed, matching the calculation); an ad hoc patch typically explains only the exact anomaly it was invented to explain and nothing more.

What this means for "the data disproved it"

Between underdetermination and the Duhem-Quine problem, two chapters have now shown that neither confirmation nor refutation is ever quite as clean as Chapter 1's simple falsification story suggested. A theory surviving a test doesn't uniquely prove it correct (Chapter 2). A theory failing a test doesn't uniquely prove it wrong (this chapter) — it just shows something, somewhere in a large bundle of assumptions, needs revision, and figuring out exactly what takes further scientific judgment, not pure logic. This isn't a flaw unique to science, and it isn't a reason to distrust well-tested theories broadly — it's a more accurate, more honest picture of what a "failed prediction" is actually telling you at the moment it happens.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Next time a prediction of yours fails — a plan, a forecast, a bet on how something would turn out — list every auxiliary assumption bundled into it, not just the core idea, before deciding what was actually wrong.
  • Notice the difference between a real fix and an ad hoc patch in your own reasoning: when you revise a belief after being wrong, does the revision predict anything new, or does it only exist to explain away the one thing that went wrong?
