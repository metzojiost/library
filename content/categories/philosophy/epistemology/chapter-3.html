Chapter 1's Gettier problems and Chapter 2's regress problem share a hidden assumption: that justification is a matter of having the right beliefs supporting other beliefs. Reliabilism rejects that assumption at the root, and in doing so, offers one of the cleaner escapes from the Gettier problem that decades of patching the traditional definition never quite achieved.

A completely different question: not "why do you believe it," but "how did the belief get made"

Philosopher Alvin Goldman, developing the view through the 1960s and 70s, proposed that a belief counts as knowledge not because of some chain of supporting reasons, but because it was produced by a reliable process (a belief-forming method that tends to produce true beliefs — vision under normal lighting, sound memory, valid logical reasoning — as opposed to one that's error-prone, like a broken clock or a habit of guessing). Your belief that it's raining, formed by looking out the window in good light with functioning eyes, counts as knowledge under reliabilism not because you can articulate an internal justification for trusting your eyes, but simply because vision, under those conditions, reliably produces accurate beliefs about the weather. This is a genuinely external test — it doesn't require the believer to be able to introspect or defend their own reasoning at all, which is exactly what separates it from foundationalism and coherentism, both of which are built around what beliefs a person can internally point to as support.

How this dissolves the stopped clock, cleanly

Go back to Chapter 1's Gettier case: glancing at a stopped clock that happens to display the correct time, and forming a justified true belief that turns out to be lucky rather than genuine knowledge. Reliabilism handles this case almost effortlessly. A stopped clock is not a reliable process for telling time — it's right exactly once every twelve hours, and wrong the rest of the time, so any belief it produces about the current time, even a true one, wasn't generated by a reliable method. The belief fails to count as knowledge not because of some subtle internal justification gap, but simply because the process that produced it is untrustworthy as a general method, full stop — which is precisely why the case felt like luck rather than knowledge in the first place. This is reliabilism's biggest selling point: instead of trying to patch the definition of "justification" to somehow exclude lucky cases while still counting normal ones, it just asks a more direct empirical question about the belief-forming process itself.

Where reliabilism runs into its own trouble

The theory's biggest weakness is called the generality problem: any single belief-forming instance can be described as an instance of many different processes at different levels of specificity, and reliability can shift wildly depending on which description you pick. Was a particular belief produced by "vision" (generally quite reliable), "vision in low light at a distance" (much less reliable), or "vision of this exact object under these exact lighting conditions on this exact day" (a category so narrow it's either 100% or 0% reliable, since it only ever happened once)? Reliabilism needs some principled way to pick the right level of description to evaluate, and nobody has produced a fully satisfying rule for choosing it — critics argue this makes the theory dependent on an arbitrary judgment call it was supposed to avoid needing in the first place. A separate worry, sometimes called the clairvoyance problem, points out that a genuinely, secretly reliable but totally unverifiable process (imagine someone with real, working clairvoyance they have no idea they possess, and no reason to trust) would count as producing knowledge under strict reliabilism — which strikes many people as backwards, since the person has no internal grounds at all for confidence in what they're sensing.

What reliabilism gets right regardless

Even with these unresolved problems, reliabilism captures something real that foundationalism and coherentism both tend to underplay: most of what actually makes ordinary perception, memory, and reasoning trustworthy has very little to do with anyone's ability to articulate a justification for it, and everything to do with those faculties simply working correctly, most of the time, as a matter of biological and cognitive fact. A small child correctly believes there's a dog in the yard by seeing it, with no capacity whatsoever to construct a philosophical justification — and it would be strange to deny that qualifies as knowledge purely on those grounds. Reliabilism takes that intuition and builds an entire theory of knowledge directly around it.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Pick a belief you hold and ask the reliabilist question instead of the traditional one: not "can I justify this," but "was the process that produced this belief actually reliable, historically, under these conditions?"
  • Try the generality problem on your own reasoning: take a recent belief and describe the process that produced it at three different levels of specificity — notice how differently reliable each description sounds.
