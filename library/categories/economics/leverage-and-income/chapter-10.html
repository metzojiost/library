Chapter 10: Auditing Your Own Leverage

~3 min read

Nine chapters have covered nine distinct mechanisms: the four types of leverage and BATNA (Chapter 1), why capital compounds faster than labor (Chapter 2), the real math of compounding (Chapter 3), which skills actually compound (Chapter 4), anchoring and information asymmetry in negotiation (Chapter 5), equity and expected value (Chapter 6), debt's symmetric amplification (Chapter 7), the media flywheel (Chapter 8), and how leverage fails (Chapter 9). This closing chapter isn't new material — it's a practical framework for actually using all nine at once, on your own specific situation.

Step 1: Separate your income into what scales and what doesn't

Going back to Chapter 1's core distinction: for each source of income or value you currently generate, ask whether it's capped at (hours) × (rate), or whether it's decoupled from your hours in some way — through capital (Chapter 2), equity (Chapter 6), or something reusable like code or media (Chapter 8). Most people's income is entirely the first kind. That's not a failure to be corrected immediately — it's simply the honest starting map that everything else in this framework gets built on top of.

Step 2: Identify your actual scarce combination

From Chapter 4: what specific combination of individually-solid skills do you occupy that few other people share? This is usually not obvious from inside your own head, because the individual pieces feel unremarkable to you precisely because you already have them — it's often more visible from the outside, through what people specifically come to you for.

Step 3: Audit your BATNA in the relationships that actually matter

From Chapter 1 and Chapter 5: in your current job, your current major client or business relationship, or any recurring negotiation in your life, what genuinely happens if it falls through? If the honest answer is "not much changes because I have real alternatives," that's real, quiet leverage already in place. If it's "everything changes," that's the single highest-value thing to work on before the next time that relationship needs renegotiating.

Step 4: Size any leverage you're using to how wrong you can afford to be

From Chapter 9, the one rule that overrides all the others: for any capital, debt, or equity exposure currently in your life, what's the actual downside scenario, and can you survive it without a forced, badly-timed exit? This question comes last in the framework, but it's the one that should override every other answer if the honest response is no.

None of these four steps require predicting markets, timing anything, or being unusually talented — which was the whole point of grounding this topic in mechanisms rather than in the market-forecasting, competitor-modeling side of economics. Leverage, negotiated well and sized honestly, is available to work with regardless of where you're starting from.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Actually run all four steps, in order, on your current situation, in writing — not as a mental exercise, but as four short written answers. The value of this framework is in seeing all four answers laid out together, not any one of them in isolation.
