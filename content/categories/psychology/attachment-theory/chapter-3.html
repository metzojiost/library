Chapter 2 ended with a question worth taking literally: Bowlby proposed that early attachment experience gets built into a mental "model" the child then carries forward. But how do you actually study something like that in an adult, decades after their own infancy, when nobody can rerun the Strange Situation on a 40-year-old? Psychologist Mary Main, one of Ainsworth's own former students, built exactly that tool — and the way it works turns out to be more surprising than a simple questionnaire.

The internal working model, defined precisely

Bowlby's internal working model (a largely unconscious mental template, built from repeated early experience with caregivers, that shapes expectations about whether other people can be relied on for comfort and safety) isn't a single memory or a belief someone could easily state out loud — it operates more like an assumption running quietly in the background, shaping how ambiguous situations get interpreted before conscious reasoning even engages. A securely-attached model defaults to: distress is manageable, and reaching out for help works. An insecure model defaults to something else — that reaching out won't help (avoidant), or that reaching out is urgent and uncertain to succeed (anxious). Crucially, Bowlby argued this template, once formed, tends to actively filter new experience to fit itself: a child (or later, an adult) with an insecure model doesn't neutrally evaluate each new relationship on its own evidence — they tend to notice and remember what confirms the model, and discount or reinterpret what doesn't, which is part of why the pattern is so persistent without active, sustained counter-evidence.

The Adult Attachment Interview: not what you say, but how you say it

Mary Main, along with Carol George and Nancy Kaplan, developed the Adult Attachment Interview (AAI) in the 1980s specifically to measure this model in adults — and its central insight is genuinely counterintuitive: what predicts an adult's attachment classification isn't primarily the content of their childhood story (a difficult childhood doesn't automatically mean an insecure classification), but the coherence of how they narrate it — whether their account is organized, consistent, and emotionally connected, or whether it's vague, contradictory, defensively idealized ("my childhood was perfect, I can't remember anything specific"), or overwhelmed by unprocessed emotion mid-story. An adult who had a genuinely difficult childhood but can discuss it with clarity, emotional access, and a coherent narrative is classified as earned secure — directly validating the concept Chapter 1 introduced — while an adult with an objectively easier childhood but a fragmented, evasive, or overwhelmed way of discussing it is more likely classified as insecure. The story's content matters less than whether the internal working model has actually been processed into something coherent.

A striking, well-replicated finding: this predicts the next generation

The AAI's most consequential finding, replicated across many studies, is that a parent's own attachment classification — assessed before their child is even born — predicts, with real statistical reliability, what attachment classification that child will later show in the Strange Situation, at rates well above chance. This is sometimes called the intergenerational transmission of attachment, and it isn't simply genetic — the mechanism runs largely through sensitivity (a caregiver's ability to accurately notice and appropriately respond to a child's signals), which a coherent, well-processed internal working model makes easier to offer consistently, and a fragmented or overwhelmed one makes harder, even when the parent consciously wants to do differently. This gives Chapter 1's "earned security" concept real practical weight beyond an individual's own comfort: processing an insecure model into a coherent one is linked to a measurably different outcome for the next generation, not just a personal one.

Why "model," not "trait," is the right word

It's worth being precise about what the internal working model is and isn't, because the language can slide into sounding more fixed than the research actually supports. It's a learned, updateable template, not a hardwired personality trait — which is exactly why Chapter 1's earned security is possible at all, and why the AAI measures current narrative coherence rather than assuming a fixed classification for life. The model runs largely automatically, shaping split-second reactions and defaults, which is what makes it feel so stubbornly fixed from the inside — but the growing body of research on earned security demonstrates that sustained, different relational experience (in therapy, in a stable partnership, in any sufficiently long and consistent corrective relationship) really can update it, just not quickly or automatically.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Try narrating a difficult part of your own early life out loud or in writing, and notice the coherence, not just the content: is it organized and emotionally accessible, or do you notice vagueness, contradictions, or a rush past the hard parts?
  • Watch for your own internal working model filtering new evidence: next time a new relationship gives you a data point that contradicts your usual expectation about people, notice whether you actually update on it, or quietly explain it away.
