Pick any decision you made today — what to eat, what to click on, whether to read this sentence. Did you freely choose it, or was it the only thing you were ever going to do, given the exact state of your brain, your mood, your history, and the laws of physics acting on all of it a moment before? This is the free will problem, and despite sounding abstract, it quietly sits underneath huge chunks of law, ethics, and how we treat other people — the whole idea of "blame" assumes someone could have done otherwise.

Determinism: one long chain of causes

Determinism is the view that every event — including every thought and decision you have — is the necessary result of prior causes, according to fixed laws. Your decision to read this sentence was caused by neurons firing in a particular pattern, which was caused by earlier brain states, which trace back through your entire life, and ultimately back past your birth to causes you never had any control over. Under strict determinism, given the exact same universe rewound to a minute ago, you would make the exact same choice every single time — there was never actually a fork in the road, only the appearance of one.

A tempting escape is quantum mechanics, which contains genuine randomness at very small scales. But randomness doesn't obviously rescue free will — a decision caused by a random quantum flicker isn't controlled by you any more than one caused by rigid prior causes. A coin flip in your brain isn't freedom; it's just a different kind of thing you don't control.

Three positions, not two

Hard determinism accepts determinism and concludes free will, in any meaningful sense, is an illusion. We feel like authors of our choices because we don't have access to the underlying causes — but that feeling isn't evidence that the causes weren't there.

Libertarian free will (a metaphysical position, unrelated to libertarian politics) rejects determinism for human choices specifically. It holds that at least some decisions genuinely break the causal chain — you could have done otherwise, full stop, with everything else about the universe held exactly the same. The hard question for this view is describing what, exactly, is doing the choosing, if not some cause. An uncaused choice starts to look suspiciously like the random quantum flicker above — not obviously "free," just undetermined.

Compatibilism: redefining the word "free"

The most popular position among professional philosophers today is compatibilism. It accepts that determinism is probably true, but argues that this was never actually in conflict with free will in the first place — because we were using the word "free" wrong.

Compatibilists argue that being "free" was never about breaking the causal chain. It's about which kind of cause produced your action. If you handed over your wallet because someone held a gun to you, that wasn't free — an external force overrode your own reasons. If you handed over your wallet to a charity because you genuinely wanted to, that was free — even though "wanting to" was itself caused by your upbringing, personality, and brain chemistry. Freedom, on this view, means acting from your own desires and reasoning, without external coercion (being forced by someone or something outside you) — regardless of what caused those desires to exist in the first place.

Critics of compatibilism call this a "cheap" redefinition — it gives you a word that still says "free," but quietly empties out the thing most people actually cared about when they asked whether they could have done otherwise. Compatibilists respond that the alternative — uncaused, random choices — was never a coherent thing to want in the first place.

None of the three positions has decisively won. What's genuinely useful about the debate is that it forces you to notice that "free will" was never one single, agreed-upon idea — it's at least three different claims wearing the same two words.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Notice it next time you're angry at someone's choice: ask whether your reaction assumes "they could have done otherwise" — and whether that assumption survives determinism, or only survives in the compatibilist sense (acting from their own reasons, uncoerced).
  • Try tracing one impulsive decision today — what to eat, what to click — backward through mood, environment, and habit. See how far back you can find a cause before the choice stops feeling "free" to you.
