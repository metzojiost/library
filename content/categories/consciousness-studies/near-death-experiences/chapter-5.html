Chapter 1 introduced the skeptical physiological explanations briefly: REM intrusion, ketamine-like dissociative effects, and oxygen deprivation. This closing chapter gives the strongest of these — REM intrusion specifically — the detailed treatment it deserves, and weighs the full physiological case honestly against what it can and can't actually account for.

Nelson's REM intrusion research

Neurologist Kevin Nelson led the most systematic research program testing whether NDE-prone individuals share an underlying neurological trait, publishing a striking 2006 finding: people who had experienced an NDE reported significantly higher rates, across their entire lives, of REM intrusion (episodes where REM-sleep-associated processes — muscle paralysis, vivid hallucinatory imagery — occur while a person is awake or in a borderline waking state, rather than confined to actual sleep, closely related to the sleep paralysis phenomenon covered elsewhere in this library) than a comparable control group who hadn't had an NDE — including higher rates of ordinary sleep paralysis and hypnagogic hallucinations reported from well before their near-death event, not only afterward. Nelson's proposed explanation: some individuals may have a nervous system with a lower threshold for REM-related processes intruding into waking consciousness generally, and that same underlying trait, activated by the extreme physiological stress of a genuine medical crisis, could plausibly produce the specific tunnel, light, and floating-sensation content Chapter 1 catalogued, borrowing directly from the same neural machinery that produces those elements during ordinary sleep-paralysis episodes.

Why this is a genuinely strong piece of the physiological case

What makes Nelson's finding more compelling than a simple "NDEs are just a kind of hallucination" dismissal is its specificity and its predictive structure: it doesn't just assert a general physiological cause after the fact — it identifies a measurable, independently-verifiable trait (REM intrusion proneness, testable through a standard sleep-history questionnaire, unrelated to the NDE itself) and predicts that people with more of that trait should show up more often among NDE-havers, a prediction his data actually confirmed. This is a meaningfully stronger form of evidence than pure post-hoc explanation, because it makes a testable claim about who is more likely to have this kind of experience, checkable independent of the content of any specific NDE account itself, and the check came back positive.

What even the strongest physiological case still doesn't fully close

In the same spirit of precision this topic has maintained throughout, it's worth being exact about what Nelson's finding does and doesn't establish. It provides a genuine, testable, confirmed mechanism for why certain individuals might be more prone to NDE-type experiences generally, and it plausibly accounts for much of the specific sensory content (tunnel, light, floating). It doesn't, on its own, fully explain Chapter 1's hardest specific puzzle — reports of accurate awareness of events occurring during periods of flat or minimal measured brain activity, the detail that made this topic genuinely contested rather than simply a settled case of hallucination. Chapter 2's Borjigin gamma-surge finding adds a further, complementary piece specifically addressing the timing puzzle, but the full physiological account still requires assembling several separate, individually well-evidenced pieces (REM intrusion proneness, the gamma surge, oxygen deprivation's visual effects) into a combined explanation that hasn't yet been demonstrated, end to end, as fully sufficient in one single, integrated study.

Closing the topic honestly

Five chapters have moved from the initial framing of two live, unresolved positions (Chapter 1), through a specific, testable mechanism for one component (Chapter 2), through durable and independently valuable aftereffects evidence (Chapter 3), through a cross-cultural pattern supporting a real, shared, biologically-rooted core (Chapter 4), to the strongest, most rigorously tested physiological model available (this chapter). The honest final position, consistent with how this topic opened: the physiological case has grown substantially stronger and more specific with each individual piece of evidence covered across this topic, without yet assembling into one fully complete, demonstrated account of every reported detail — meaning van Lommel's and Fischer's original disagreement from Chapter 1 remains real, even as the specific territory each side has to defend has narrowed considerably since either of their original positions were first staked out.
