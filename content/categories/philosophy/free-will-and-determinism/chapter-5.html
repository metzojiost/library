Four chapters have covered a genuinely unresolved philosophical debate. The legal system doesn't have the luxury of leaving it unresolved — every single day, courts have to decide who is and isn't responsible for what they did, and hand out real consequences based on that decision. This closing chapter looks at how law actually handles a question philosophy hasn't settled, and what its working answer reveals.

The law is quietly, thoroughly compatibilist

Criminal law almost never asks whether a defendant's action was ultimately caused by prior events all the way back — under strict determinism, of course it was, for every defendant, in every case, which would make the question useless as a legal test even if judges wanted to ask it. Instead, the law asks a narrower, practical version of the free-will question, built almost exactly on the compatibilist distinction from Chapter 1: did the person act from their own reasoning and intent, free of external coercion or a compromised capacity to understand what they were doing? This shows up directly in the concept of mens rea (Latin for "guilty mind" — the legal requirement that a person acted with a culpable mental state, such as intent or recklessness, not merely that their body performed a harmful act). A person who hands over their wallet at gunpoint and a person who hands it over freely to a mugger who merely asked politely committed the same physical act, but the law (and everyone's moral sense) treats them completely differently — not based on whether either action was "truly" free of all prior causation, but based on whether it flowed from the person's own uncoerced will, exactly the compatibilist test from Chapter 1, running quietly underneath the entire structure of criminal law without ever needing to cite a philosopher.

Where the excuses actually live — and how narrow they are

The formal legal excuses and defenses map closely onto Peter Strawson's account from Chapter 3, not onto global metaphysics. Duress excuses someone forced to act under an immediate, credible threat — an external override of their own will, similar to Strawson's coercion exemption. Insanity defenses, in most jurisdictions, require showing the defendant lacked the capacity to understand the nature or wrongfulness of their act, not merely that some abnormal brain process caused their behavior (every behavior, sane or not, is caused by some brain process) — the legal test tracks capacity for reasoning, not causal history. Age-based exemptions for children track a similar developmental-capacity logic. None of these defenses ask "was determinism true when this happened" — they ask much narrower, practical questions about coercion, capacity, and understanding, exactly the situational exemptions Strawson identified as what responsibility was actually tracking all along.

Where neuroscience has started pushing into court, carefully

Findings like Chapter 2's readiness-potential research and modern neuroimaging have started entering courtrooms, mostly around sentencing rather than guilt itself — a brain tumor pressing on regions tied to impulse control, for instance, has been introduced as mitigating evidence in real cases, most famously in a well-documented 2000s case involving a man whose sudden onset of pedophilic behavior was linked to a tumor and resolved after its removal. But courts have been notably cautious about letting this slide into a general "my brain made me do it" defense — because, again, every brain state causes every behavior, sane or not, so a specific finding has to show something narrower and more targeted: an identifiable impairment to the specific capacities (impulse control, understanding consequences, distinguishing right from wrong) the law already cares about — not a philosophical argument about determinism in general, which courts have consistently and explicitly declined to treat as a defense on its own.

What five chapters add up to

This topic opened with a question that sounds purely abstract — could you have done otherwise? — and ends somewhere much more concrete: whichever metaphysical position turns out to be correct, human institutions, and most people's actual day-to-day moral reactions, already run on something closer to Strawson's and the compatibilists' answer than on the strict libertarian one. That doesn't settle the metaphysics. Chapters 1, 2, and 4 leave real, live disagreement standing. But it does mean the practical stakes of the debate are smaller than the popular framing ("if determinism is true, nothing matters") usually suggests — the concepts doing the actual work in courtrooms, friendships, and everyday judgments turn out to be more resilient to the metaphysical uncertainty than they first appear.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Pick a recent news story involving a criminal defense or mitigation argument and see whether it's actually built on duress, capacity, or understanding — the law's real categories — rather than a broader claim about free will.
  • Notice your own private "sentencing" of people in daily life — how quickly you shift from anger to something softer once you learn about coercion, incapacity, or circumstances you hadn't known. That shift is the same logic the legal system formalizes.
