Chapter 2 covered the informed side proving itself voluntarily. This chapter is the mirror image: the uninformed side, instead of waiting to be convinced, designs the deal itself so that the informed side's own choices give away what they know — whether or not they'd have volunteered it. This is called screening (the uninformed party offers a menu of options, structured so that different hidden types rationally sort themselves into different choices, revealing type through the choice itself), and it's arguably the more commercially important of the two mechanisms, because it doesn't require the informed side's cooperation at all — just their ordinary self-interest.

Rothschild and Stiglitz: making an insurance menu do the sorting

Economists Michael Rothschild and Joseph Stiglitz worked out the classic version of this in a 1976 paper on competitive insurance markets. An insurer can't directly observe whether an applicant is high-risk or low-risk — exactly the problem from Chapter 1 — but instead of trying to investigate each applicant individually, it can offer two different contracts and let applicants sort themselves: a low-premium policy with a high deductible, and a high-premium policy with a low (or no) deductible. A low-risk applicant, who doesn't expect to file many claims, prefers paying less up front and accepting more exposure if something rare does happen — the high-deductible option is the better deal for them. A high-risk applicant, who realistically expects to need coverage, is willing to pay more up front to avoid a large deductible when the claim actually comes. Nobody has to declare their risk level. The choice itself, made in each applicant's own rational self-interest, sorts the pool — this is a separating equilibrium (a menu of options structured so that different hidden types rationally choose different options, without anyone needing to disclose their type directly).

Screening devices that don't look like menus

Once you're looking for it, screening shows up in places that don't resemble a formal insurance contract at all. A classic case, described by Stiglitz in earlier work on price discrimination: mail-in rebates and clippable coupons offer the same product at two effective prices, but only to whoever is willing to spend the time and effort to use them. That effort is a screen — price-sensitive customers, for whom the savings are worth the hassle, use the coupon; less price-sensitive customers don't bother, and pay full price without anyone needing to ask their income or willingness to pay directly. Airlines do something structurally similar with fare restrictions: a ticket that's cheaper but requires a Saturday-night stay and is non-refundable screens for leisure travelers (who don't mind the restriction and are price-sensitive) versus business travelers (who need flexibility and whose employer is typically paying anyway), letting the airline charge each group closer to what they're actually willing to pay without ever asking "are you traveling for work?" A "students and seniors" discount that requires showing ID is a screen against everyone who isn't in the target group and could otherwise just claim the lower price.

What makes a screen actually work

A screening menu only sorts correctly if the options are built around a real difference in preferences or costs between the types being separated — the same underlying requirement that makes signaling credible in Chapter 2. The insurance deductible works because low-risk and high-risk people genuinely value the same trade-off differently. If both types had identical preferences over the two contracts, offering a menu wouldn't sort anyone — everyone would just pick whichever option was objectively better, revealing nothing. Designing a working screen means finding the specific dimension where the hidden types would make genuinely different choices even without knowing they were being sorted — which is why real-world screens (deductibles, restrictions, required effort) tend to track exactly the underlying trait the screener actually cares about, rather than being arbitrary hoops.

The limits: screening can leave money on the table, or fail outright

Rothschild and Stiglitz's own result had a sharper edge than "menus work": they showed that under some conditions, no stable separating menu exists at all — competition between insurers can push the market toward offering only a single pooled contract, or toward no equilibrium existing at all, depending on the underlying mix of risk types. Screening is a genuinely useful tool, not a guaranteed fix. And even when it does work, it can be visibly inefficient in a way people notice and resent: the low-risk applicant in the insurance example is getting less coverage than they'd ideally want, purely so that their choice is distinguishable from the high-risk applicant's — a cost paid specifically to make sorting possible, not because less coverage is otherwise desirable. That's the same trade-off Chapter 2 flagged for signaling, from the other side of the table: separating hidden types from each other is rarely free, whichever side is doing the separating.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Next time you see a menu of options for the same underlying product or service — pricing tiers, deductible choices, "basic vs. pro" plans — ask what hidden trait about you each option is designed to reveal through which one you'd naturally pick.
  • Notice a "hoop" you've had to jump through recently — a rebate, a waiting period, a form, a trial period — and consider whether its actual function is to screen out people it wasn't built for, rather than to inconvenience everyone equally.
