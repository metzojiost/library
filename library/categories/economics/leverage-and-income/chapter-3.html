Chapter 3: The Real Math of Compounding

~3 min read

Chapter 2 showed that a persistent rate advantage (r > g) compounds into a large gap over time. This chapter opens up what "compounds" actually means mechanically — because the intuition most people carry around for it is quietly wrong, in a way that changes real decisions.

Why intuition underestimates compound growth

The human brain is naturally wired for linear thinking — if something grows by a fixed amount each period, the total after N periods is easy to estimate by eye. Exponential growth — where each period's growth is a percentage of an already-grown base, not a fixed amount — is systematically underestimated by almost everyone, even people who understand the formula perfectly well on paper. The classic demonstration: fold a piece of paper in half 42 times (impossible physically, but imagine it), and its thickness reaches the Moon. Nobody's gut-level intuition gets anywhere close to that number, because doubling looks unremarkable for the first dozen steps and only becomes visually dramatic near the end — which is exactly the shape of compound growth in a bank account, an investment, or a skill practiced consistently: unremarkable for years, then abruptly large.

The Rule of 72

A useful mental shortcut: divide 72 by an annual growth rate (as a percentage) to estimate how many years it takes to double. At 6% annual growth, money doubles in roughly 12 years (72 ÷ 6). At 9%, roughly 8 years. At 12%, roughly 6 years. This isn't just a finance trick — it applies to anything that compounds: a skill improving 5% a year, a user base growing 15% a month, a habit's effects accumulating steadily. The rule makes an otherwise-abstract growth rate concrete: "9% a year" doesn't feel like much in one year, but "doubles roughly every 8 years, then doubles again" reframes the same number into something the intuition can actually grip.

Why time in the market beats timing the market

A direct, practical consequence of exponential growth: interrupting a compounding process, even briefly, costs disproportionately more than the interruption itself would suggest, because it doesn't just lose that period's growth — it delays every subsequent doubling by that same amount. This is the actual mathematical reason behind the well-worn investing advice to stay invested through downturns rather than trying to sell before drops and buy back before rises: missing even a handful of the market's best days, historically, done in an attempt to dodge its worst days, tends to sharply cut long-run compounded returns, since the best and worst days cluster close together in time and are essentially impossible to predict in advance. The same logic applies to non-financial compounding too — a skill, an audience, a business relationship — interruptions cost more than the missed period alone.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Pick something in your life that compounds — savings, a skill, a relationship, a habit — and estimate its "growth rate," then apply the Rule of 72: does the resulting doubling time change how patient or impatient you feel about it?
