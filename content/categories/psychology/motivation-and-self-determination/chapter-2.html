Chapter 1 mentioned, in its final hook, a daycare that started fining parents for late pickups and saw late pickups increase rather than decrease. That single, striking result is actually part of a well-developed body of economic and psychological research on exactly when and why adding an incentive backfires — and the mechanism behind it turns out to be a direct, practical extension of Chapter 1's overjustification effect, running at the level of entire institutions rather than individual hobbies.

The daycare study, in full

Economists Uri Gneezy and Aldo Rustichini ran this as a real field experiment across ten Israeli daycare centers in 2000. For the first weeks, there was no fine for late pickup — parents were simply expected, socially, to arrive on time, and most did, motivated by an implicit sense of consideration for the staff who had to stay late. The centers then introduced a modest financial fine for pickups more than ten minutes late. Late pickups didn't decrease. They roughly doubled, and stayed elevated even after the fine was later removed — parents who had recategorized lateness as a paid service didn't revert to their earlier sense of social obligation once the price tag disappeared. Gneezy and Rustichini's explanation: before the fine, lateness violated an implicit social and moral norm (being inconsiderate of the staff's time). After the fine, lateness became a transaction with an explicit price — parents could simply buy the right to be late, and many did, because paying a fee removed the guilt that had previously been the real, informal cost of lateness.

Naming the broader pattern: motivation crowding theory

Motivation crowding theory, developed substantially by economist Bruno Frey, generalizes this finding: introducing an extrinsic incentive (money, a fine, a formal reward) for a behavior previously governed by intrinsic or social motivation can crowd out that original motivation rather than adding to it — and once crowded out, the original motivation frequently doesn't fully return even if the extrinsic incentive is later removed, exactly as the daycare parents' behavior demonstrated. This isn't a claim that incentives never work — for purely mechanical tasks with no meaningful intrinsic motivation to begin with, extrinsic incentives reliably increase output, largely without this crowding-out problem, which is why Chapter 1 was careful to note that carrot-and-stick incentives work reasonably well for simple, repetitive tasks specifically. The crowding-out risk is concentrated exactly where Chapter 1 already flagged concern: tasks that carry real intrinsic, social, or moral meaning before any payment enters the picture.

A second, well-documented case: paying for blood donation

Sociologist Richard Titmuss argued as early as 1970, largely on theoretical grounds, that paying donors for blood could reduce total donations rather than increase them, by displacing the altruistic motivation that drove unpaid donation with a much weaker, purely transactional one. Decades later, economists Carl Mellström and Magnus Johannesson tested this directly in a controlled 2008 field experiment: offering payment for blood donation reduced donation rates among women specifically, compared to a no-payment control group, even though the same offer had no significant effect among men — a striking, gender-specific confirmation that adding money to an act with real prior social and altruistic meaning can measurably shrink the pool of people willing to do it, not just fail to grow it. Crucially, the effect reversed when donors were given the option to redirect the payment to charity rather than keep it themselves — restoring roughly the original, unpaid donation rate — which supports the crowding-out mechanism specifically: it wasn't the introduction of any transaction that caused the drop, it was the introduction of a personally self-interested transaction that reframed a previously altruistic act.

What this means for designing real incentives

Taken together with Chapter 1's overjustification effect, these findings support a specific, actionable design principle rather than a blanket "never use incentives" rule: before adding an extrinsic reward to a behavior, ask honestly whether meaningful intrinsic or social motivation already exists for it. If it doesn't — a genuinely tedious, mechanical task nobody has any independent reason to enjoy — extrinsic incentives are usually safe and effective. If it does — care work, creative work, socially or morally meaningful behavior — adding a price tag risks reframing the entire activity from "something I do because I value it" into "something I do because I'm paid for it," with the specific, well-documented risk that the original motivation doesn't simply add to the new one, but gets displaced by it, sometimes permanently.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Think of a rule or system in your own life or workplace that uses a fine or fee to discourage a behavior — a late fee, a penalty clause — and ask honestly whether it might be functioning as a price tag rather than a genuine deterrent.
  • Before adding a reward to encourage someone (a child, an employee, yourself) toward a behavior that already carries some intrinsic or social meaning, pause and consider whether the reward might crowd out a motivation that was already doing the job.
