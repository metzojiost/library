Everything covered so far — the Strange Situation, Harlow's monkeys, the internal working model — was built and tested on the parent-infant bond specifically. In 1987, psychologists Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver asked a question that seems obvious in hindsight but hadn't been formally tested: does the same basic framework describe adult romantic love? Their answer reshaped how relationship psychology gets studied, and it started with a newspaper.

The "Love Quiz" that launched a research field

Hazan and Shaver published a short questionnaire in a local newspaper, describing three paragraphs meant to correspond to Ainsworth's secure, anxious, and avoidant categories, and asked readers to pick which one best matched how they generally experienced romantic relationships. The proportions that came back — roughly matching the proportions Ainsworth had found in infants decades earlier — were the first hint that something structurally similar was really going on. Follow-up research, using far more rigorous methods than a newspaper quiz, confirmed the pattern held: adults' self-reported attachment style predicted real, measurable differences in how they behaved in relationships — how they handled conflict, how comfortable they were with intimacy, how they responded when a partner was distressed, and how they themselves sought comfort when upset.

What each style actually looks like in adult partnership

The adult versions of Chapter 1's categories carry recognizable, if messier, real-world signatures. A securely attached adult tends to communicate needs relatively directly, can tolerate a partner's need for independence without reading it as rejection, and can offer comfort without it triggering their own anxiety. An anxiously attached adult tends to seek high levels of closeness and reassurance, is highly sensitive to small signs of distance, and can slip into what researchers call protest behavior (escalating attempts — increased calling, texting, or conflict — to reestablish a caregiver's or partner's attention when connection feels threatened), a direct adult echo of an anxiously attached infant's distress at separation. An avoidantly attached adult tends to prize independence, can feel smothered by a partner's bids for closeness, and often withdraws — emotionally or physically — exactly when intimacy intensifies, mirroring the avoidant infant's suppressed-but-still-present physiological stress response from Chapter 1.

The anxious-avoidant trap

One particular pairing has received outsized research and clinical attention because of how self-reinforcing it tends to be: an anxiously attached partner's escalating bids for reassurance can feel, to an avoidantly attached partner, exactly like the engulfment their own model is primed to avoid — prompting withdrawal, which the anxious partner's model reads as exactly the abandonment it's primed to fear, prompting more intense protest behavior, which drives further withdrawal. Neither partner is doing anything most people would call malicious; each is responding, largely automatically, out of the internal working model Chapter 3 described, in a way that consistently confirms the other partner's worst expectation. Couples therapists trained in attachment-informed approaches (such as Emotionally Focused Therapy, developed by psychologist Sue Johnson) work explicitly with this cycle — not by asking either partner to simply try harder, but by helping each partner recognize the automatic protest-and-withdraw pattern for what it is, in real time, so it stops running unexamined.

Style is a tendency, not a verdict on a relationship's chances

It's worth resisting a tempting oversimplification here: knowing your own or a partner's attachment style isn't a diagnosis or a compatibility score. Chapter 3 already established that internal working models can differ somewhat by relationship and can shift with sustained, different experience — earned security applies here directly. An anxiously and avoidantly attached couple isn't fated to repeat the cycle above forever; awareness of the specific mechanism, plus consistent counter-evidence from a partner willing to respond differently than the model expects, is exactly the kind of sustained corrective experience that research on earned security shows can genuinely update the pattern over time, for both partners at once.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Notice your own protest or withdrawal pattern the next time you feel distant from a partner or close friend: do you lean toward escalating contact, or toward pulling back — and can you name it as a pattern rather than just reacting to it?
  • If you recognize the anxious-avoidant cycle in a relationship of your own, try naming it out loud to the other person — "I think I'm doing the protest thing right now" — which research on this cycle suggests can interrupt it more effectively than either partner trying to push through it alone.
