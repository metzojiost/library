Chapter 1 covered what sleep does once it starts. This chapter covers a different, equally important question: why does falling asleep come so easily for some people and so badly for others, even with identical opportunity and identical exhaustion?

Hyperarousal: a measurable state, not a character flaw

Sleep researchers use the term hyperarousal to describe a documented physiological state in people who struggle to fall asleep: elevated heart rate, higher body temperature, increased stress hormone levels, and — measurable directly on EEG — more high-frequency beta wave (fast brain-wave activity associated with alert, active waking thought — the opposite of the slow waves that dominate deep sleep) activity at bedtime, compared to people who fall asleep easily. This is a real, physically measurable difference, not simply "bad habits" or "not being tired enough" — it's closer to the nervous system running an alert-mode idle speed that doesn't downshift as readily as it does for other people, even when everything else (exhaustion, bedtime, environment) is controlled for.

Cognitive versus somatic arousal

Researchers further split this into two related but distinguishable types. Somatic arousal is physical — a racing heart, muscle tension, restlessness in the body itself. Cognitive arousal is mental — racing thoughts, replaying the day, planning tomorrow, a mind that keeps generating new threads exactly when it's supposed to be quieting down. Most people who describe themselves as unable to "turn their brain off" at bedtime are describing cognitive arousal specifically, and it's worth noting this connects directly back to Chapter 1's memory consolidation process: falling asleep is partly the brain handing off the day's unprocessed material to be consolidated during sleep itself. A mind that's still actively, consciously working through that same material at bedtime is arguably doing a live, effortful version of a job that's supposed to run automatically, unconsciously, later — which would help explain why it feels so specifically effortful and unresolved, rather than just generally restless.

Why "just relax" doesn't work, and what actually does

This framing matters practically, not just academically: telling someone with genuine cognitive hyperarousal to "just relax" is a bit like telling someone with a genuinely fast-running clock to "just slow down" through willpower alone — the state is measurably real, not a simple choice being stubbornly refused. What the research-backed treatments actually target is specific and structural, not vague relaxation advice: offloading the racing cognitive content earlier in the evening, rather than at the exact moment sleep is supposed to start, and building strong environmental and behavioral associations between "bed" and "sleep" specifically, rather than "bed" and "wired, active thinking." Both of these become the practical core of Chapter 5's toolkit — the point of this chapter is establishing that the problem is real and specific enough to have a real, specific answer, not a character trait to just push through.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Notice which type of arousal you're actually experiencing tonight: as you're trying to fall asleep, check whether it's more physical (restless body, can't get comfortable) or more mental (thoughts looping) — they're different problems, and naming which one you're facing is the first useful step.
  • Try externalizing one racing thought before bed: if your mind keeps returning to an unfinished task or worry, write one sentence about it on paper before lying down — a small, low-effort test of whether offloading it earlier reduces how much it shows up once you're trying to sleep.
