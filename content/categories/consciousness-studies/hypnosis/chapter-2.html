Chapter 1 mentioned, briefly, that hypnotic susceptibility is a fairly stable individual trait — roughly 10-15% of people highly responsive, a similar share almost entirely unresponsive, most people somewhere in between. This deserves fuller treatment, because the measurement behind that claim is unusually rigorous by the standards of psychological trait research, and what predicts high susceptibility turns out to connect directly to real, measurable brain differences.

Measuring susceptibility with real precision

Psychologists André Weitzenhoffer and Ernest Hilgard developed the Stanford Hypnotic Susceptibility Scale in the late 1950s, still in active use today alongside closely related instruments, which administers a standardized hypnotic induction followed by a fixed sequence of specific suggested tasks of increasing difficulty — from simple motor suggestions (an arm feeling too heavy to lift) through more demanding perceptual and cognitive ones (a suggested hallucination, a post-hypnotic amnesia instruction) — and scores a person's objectively observed response to each, rather than relying on self-report alone. This gives hypnotic susceptibility an unusually well-validated, objectively scored measurement compared to many personality and trait constructs, and decades of repeated testing have found scores to be remarkably stable within individuals over time — someone who scores highly susceptible in their twenties will very likely score similarly decades later, a degree of long-term stability that rivals or exceeds what Chapter 2 of this library's Big Five topic found for major personality traits.

What actually predicts high susceptibility

Despite decades of research, susceptibility correlates only weakly, or not at all, with many traits that intuition suggests should matter — general intelligence, gullibility, or overall suggestibility to non-hypnotic social pressure all show little to no meaningful relationship with hypnotic susceptibility specifically. What does correlate consistently is a trait called absorption (a stable personality trait describing the tendency to become fully, deeply engaged in an experience — a book, a film, a piece of music — to the point of tuning out surrounding reality), measured independently through its own validated questionnaire. People who score high on absorption — who report regularly losing themselves completely in a novel, a piece of music, or their own imagination — are measurably more likely to score as highly hypnotically susceptible, a finding that fits neatly with Chapter 1's reframing of hypnosis as a form of unusually focused, absorbed attention rather than a categorically separate trance state.

What brain imaging finds in highly susceptible people

Neuroscientist David Spiegel and colleagues found real, measurable differences in brain connectivity between highly susceptible and low-susceptible individuals, even outside of active hypnosis — specifically involving stronger functional connectivity between the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (involved in executive control and self-monitoring) and the salience network (involved in filtering which internal and external signals get prioritized for conscious attention). The proposed interpretation connects directly to Chapter 1's suggestion-effect findings: highly susceptible individuals appear to have a baseline neural architecture that makes it easier for executive control to hand off direct, effortless influence over perception and attention to a specific suggestion — consistent with reports from highly susceptible individuals themselves, who often describe hypnotic suggestions as requiring little or no conscious effort to experience, more like something simply happening to them than something they're actively working to produce.

Whether susceptibility is trainable — the more contested question

Given how stable susceptibility scores are over time, a genuinely practical question follows: can a low-susceptibility person become more responsive with training or practice? The evidence here is more mixed and more modest than for the underlying trait's stability. Some studies have found small, short-term increases in measured susceptibility following specific training protocols aimed at building absorption and reducing critical, analytical resistance to suggestion, but these gains tend to be modest in size and their durability over the longer term is less well established than the core trait's own stability. The most defensible current summary is that susceptibility behaves like many other measured psychological traits covered elsewhere in this library — a real, largely stable individual difference, with some limited room for modest, situational shift, rather than either a completely fixed capacity or a fully trainable skill anyone could master with sufficient practice.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Notice your own absorption tendency: do you regularly lose yourself completely in a book, film, or piece of music, tuning out your surroundings? That trait is the strongest known predictor of hypnotic susceptibility.
  • If you've ever tried hypnosis (clinical, recreational, or self-hypnosis) and it didn't seem to "work," consider that this may reflect your own baseline trait level rather than the specific technique or practitioner being at fault.
