Chapter 4 covered dream control as a graded, trainable skill, closely tied to expectation. It left one specific, genuinely strange piece out: what happens when a lucid dreamer doesn't just reshape the environment, but stops and asks another person in the dream a real question — and gets an answer nobody consciously planned.

Tholey's interviews with the people who live in dreams

German researcher Paul Tholey, one of the earliest scientists to take lucid dream control seriously as a research method, ran a striking series of experiments starting in the 1980s: instructing experienced lucid dreamers to deliberately seek out a dream character and interview them — asking their name, their thoughts, questions the dreamer didn't consciously know the answer to in advance. A meaningful share of reported responses were genuinely surprising to the dreamers themselves: answers that felt spontaneous, sometimes clever, occasionally unsettling, arriving with none of the deliberate, effortful quality of actively composing a response the way you would while awake.

Two honest interpretations, and no settled answer

There are two genuinely competing explanations, and it's worth being direct that neither has won decisively. The more conservative interpretation: a single dreaming mind is not fully transparent to itself, and is entirely capable of generating novel, surprising content the way any improvising storyteller sometimes surprises even themselves — the dreamer is still authoring every word, just without full conscious access to how. The more provocative interpretation, which Tholey himself leaned toward, is that dream characters might reflect something closer to semi-independent sub-processes within the sleeping mind — not separate people, but not simply a single, fully unified narrator either, closer to distinct streams of the same mind operating with a degree of genuine autonomy from the "main" dreaming self asking the questions.

Modern cognitive science tends to favor a version of the first explanation, partly because it doesn't require positing anything mysterious beyond well-established facts about how much of ordinary waking cognition is already unconscious and non-transparent to the conscious "you" experiencing it — most of what any brain does, awake or dreaming, isn't consciously authored in real time, it's generated and only partially exposed to awareness afterward. Dream characters, on this account, aren't more mysterious than the fact that you can't predict your own next sentence in a normal conversation either, just a particularly vivid demonstration of it.

Why this connects back to control

This reframes Chapter 4's point about control in a useful way: if dream characters draw on genuinely unconscious material the dreaming mind isn't fully steering in real time, that helps explain why control over other people and events inside a dream is often reported as harder and less reliable than control over the environment — you may be running up against the same limits on self-transparency that apply to your own waking mind, just made unusually visible by the dream's format.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Try Tholey's interview technique if you get a stable lucid dream: ask a dream character something you don't consciously know the answer to, and pay attention to whether the response feels composed or genuinely surprising as it arrives.
  • Notice unconscious generation while awake, as a comparison point: next time you're mid-conversation, notice that you don't consciously plan your next sentence word by word either — a small, waking-life echo of the same non-transparency dream characters make vivid.
