Four chapters have treated human language as a given, exploring how it's acquired, how it shapes perception, and how its cognitive effects have been over- and under-claimed. This closing chapter asks the question underneath all of them: what, precisely, makes human language different from animal communication — and how much of that gap has genuinely closed under scientific scrutiny, versus how much remains real and largely intact?

The ape language projects, and what they actually demonstrated

Starting in the 1960s, several high-profile research projects attempted to teach great apes forms of human-like communication, given that apes lack the vocal anatomy for human speech. Researchers Allen and Beatrix Gardner taught the chimpanzee Washoe American Sign Language, and Washoe eventually used well over a hundred distinct signs, reportedly combining them in novel short sequences. Researcher Sue Savage-Rumbaugh's work with the bonobo Kanzi went further, with Kanzi acquiring an even larger vocabulary of lexical symbols and demonstrating apparent comprehension of a considerable range of spoken English requests, including some involving novel combinations Kanzi had not been explicitly trained on. These results were genuinely striking and remain scientifically significant — but the field's own subsequent, more skeptical reanalysis found the initial, most enthusiastic interpretations had overreached in a specific, identifiable way.

Where the skeptical reanalysis actually landed

Linguist and psychologist researchers, most notably in a rigorous critical review by psychologist Herbert Terrace (who had himself run a comparable project with the chimpanzee Nim Chimpsky), found that much of what looked like grammatical combination in these apes' output was better explained by simpler mechanisms: prompting and cueing from trainers (sometimes unconscious, similar in structure to the Clever Hans effect, where a famous "counting" horse was actually reading subtle unconscious cues from its handler), extensive repetition of trainer-modeled sequences rather than spontaneous novel construction, and a strong tendency to produce more signs when more signs, of any kind, increased the likelihood of a reward, regardless of grammatical structure. Critically, none of the ape language projects produced clear, convincing evidence of recursion (the capacity to embed a phrase or clause inside another phrase or clause of the same type, potentially without a fixed limit — "the dog that chased the cat that caught the mouse" nesting one clause inside another), a structural feature that shows up early and automatically in every documented human language and that linguists Noam Chomsky, Marc Hauser, and W. Tecumseh Fitch argued, in an influential 2002 paper, might be the single narrowest, most specific capacity distinguishing the human language faculty from anything demonstrated in animal communication so far.

What genuinely remarkable animal communication does exist

None of this skepticism should be read as animal communication being simple or unremarkable — the corrected picture is more precise, not more dismissive. Vervet monkeys use distinct, functionally specific alarm calls for different predator types (a "leopard" call triggers climbing into trees, a distinct "eagle" call triggers looking up and diving into cover), demonstrating real referential communication tied to specific meanings. Bee waggle dances encode genuinely quantitative, spatial information (direction and distance to a food source) through a structured physical display, a remarkably sophisticated communication system by any measure. What's absent, even in these genuinely impressive systems, is the specific, open-ended generativity human language displays by early childhood — the capacity to produce and understand an effectively unlimited number of entirely novel sentences, most of which the speaker has never heard or produced before, through recursive combination of a finite set of elements. Real communication, real information transfer, real learned signals — but not, on current evidence, the specific recursive, generative structure Chomsky's Universal Grammar (Chapter 1) describes as uniquely, reliably human.

Closing the topic

Five chapters have moved from an innate grammatical scaffold (Chapter 1), through a precisely time-limited window for activating it (Chapter 2), to language's measurable but bounded effect on perception (Chapter 3), through a cautionary tale about a popular claim that outran its evidence (Chapter 4), and finally to the animal-communication comparison that helps triangulate what's actually unique about the human case. What survives this scrutiny is neither the maximalist claim that language determines all thought, nor the deflationary claim that human language is just a more complex version of ordinary animal signaling — it's a specific, well-evidenced picture of an innate, biologically time-bound, recursively generative capacity, layered with real but bounded downstream effects on perception and cognition, that current evidence has not found a clear equivalent to anywhere else in the animal kingdom.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Try constructing a sentence with three or four levels of embedded clauses ("the book that the woman who lives next to the store that closed last year used to own") and notice how effortlessly you can both produce and parse it — a small, direct demonstration of the recursion this chapter identifies as distinctively human.
  • Watch a video of a genuine animal communication system (vervet alarm calls, a bee dance) with this chapter's distinction in mind: notice what real information is being conveyed, and notice specifically what open-ended, novel-sentence-generating capacity still appears absent.
