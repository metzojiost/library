Chapter 3: Chess Masters and Chunking

~5 min read

Chapter 1 mentioned the chess grandmaster's five-second board-reconstruction feat briefly, as one striking illustration of mental representations. It deserves the full story, because it's one of the most carefully studied, most precisely explained phenomena in all of cognitive psychology — and understanding the actual mechanism, not just the impressive headline result, tells you something specific and useful about what expertise actually changes in the mind.

De Groot's original discovery

Dutch psychologist and chess master Adriaan de Groot ran the foundational version of this research in 1946, comparing grandmasters against strong club-level players on a memory task: view a real, mid-game chess position for a few seconds, then reconstruct it from memory. Grandmasters reconstructed the positions with striking accuracy — the vast majority of pieces placed correctly — while strong club players, genuinely skilled chess-players by ordinary standards, did considerably worse. De Groot's initial hypothesis was that grandmasters simply searched more moves ahead, more deeply, than weaker players — a difference in raw calculating power. Careful analysis of his own data complicated that story: the grandmasters weren't obviously searching further ahead in their move analysis than the club players. Something else was producing their superior performance, and it took nearly three more decades to pin down exactly what.

Chase and Simon's decisive follow-up: random positions erase the advantage

Psychologists William Chase and Herbert Simon ran the experiment that isolated the actual mechanism in 1973, adding one critical condition de Groot hadn't tested: alongside real, game-derived positions, they also showed players chess positions with the identical pieces placed randomly on the board — arrangements that could never actually arise from legal, sensible play. The result settled the question decisively: on real positions, grandmasters' recall advantage over weaker players was dramatic, replicating de Groot's finding. On random positions, that advantage nearly vanished — grandmasters performed only slightly better than club players, both groups now struggling roughly equally with meaningless arrangements. This ruled out a simple "better visual memory" or "faster raw processing" explanation, since either of those advantages should have persisted on random boards too. What grandmasters had wasn't a generically superior memory — it was a memory specifically primed to recognize game-relevant patterns, which simply had nothing to grab onto once those patterns were destroyed.

Chunking: the actual mechanism

Chase and Simon's explanation, now foundational to cognitive psychology, is called chunking (the process of grouping multiple individual pieces of information into a single, meaningful unit that can be held and retrieved in memory as one item, rather than as separate pieces). Human short-term memory can hold only a small number of independent items at once — a well-known limit is often cited as roughly seven, give or take a couple. A novice sees a chess position as roughly thirty separate, individual pieces, quickly overwhelming that limited capacity. A grandmaster, through years of exposure to game-relevant patterns, has learned to recognize entire configurations — a specific pawn structure, a common attacking formation, a familiar opening arrangement — as single, pre-packaged chunks, each one drawing on a deep, specific store of chess pattern knowledge built up over a career. Chase and Simon estimated grandmasters hold something on the order of 10,000 to 100,000 such chunks in long-term memory, each one instantly recognizable, effectively multiplying the amount of chess-relevant information that can be held in working memory at once, without requiring any greater raw memory capacity than anyone else.

Why this connects directly to earlier chapters

Chunking gives Chapter 1's "mental representations" claim and Chapter 2's practice-quality findings a precise, unified mechanism. Deliberate practice, as Chapter 1 described it, isn't simply building general cognitive horsepower — it's the process of building an ever-larger, ever more refined library of domain-specific chunks, one exposure and one piece of corrective feedback at a time, which is exactly why deliberate practice has to happen inside the specific domain being mastered and doesn't meaningfully transfer to unrelated fields. It also explains why genuine expertise looks so effortless from the outside and feels so effortless from the inside once achieved: an expert isn't consciously assembling a position piece by piece the way a novice must — they're instantly recognizing whole, pre-chunked patterns, which is precisely what frees up cognitive resources for the higher-level strategic thinking that separates a grandmaster's game from a beginner's.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Notice chunking in a skill you already have: reading is a good example — you no longer sound out individual letters, you recognize whole words and often whole phrases as single units. Try to recall learning to read, and how different that process felt.
  • In a skill you're actively building, notice what still feels like separate, effortful pieces versus what has started to feel like a single, automatic unit — that shift is chunking happening in real time.
