Chapter 5 closed on equity — an ownership stake instead of a straight salary — as a distinct form of leverage. This chapter is about how that trade actually works, and why it can outperform a bigger guaranteed paycheck over a long horizon, despite feeling obviously riskier in the moment.

What equity actually is

A salary is labor income paid for time already worked — bounded, predictable, and disconnected from how much value the work eventually creates. Equity is a share of ownership: if the thing you're building becomes more valuable, your stake becomes more valuable too, with no upper bound tied to hours worked. This is the direct, structural link back to Chapter 2's capital-versus-labor distinction — accepting equity is choosing to be paid, partly, in capital rather than pure labor income, with all of capital's higher variance and higher long-run compounding potential that implies.

Why the math can favor a risky stake over a bigger guaranteed number

The comparison people intuitively make — "this salary is definitely $X, that equity is worth $0 until proven otherwise" — is the wrong comparison. The right comparison is expected value (probability of each outcome, multiplied by its size, summed across all outcomes) weighed against personal risk tolerance, not the most-likely single outcome treated as certain. A 1% chance of equity worth $10 million has an expected value of $100,000 — which can genuinely exceed a modest salary difference, even though the single most likely outcome is that the equity ends up worth exactly $0. Whether that trade is actually worth taking depends heavily on personal circumstances: someone who can't cover their expenses without the guaranteed salary is right to weight the near-certain outcome more heavily than the expected-value math alone suggests, since a $0 outcome that also means missed rent is a much worse $0 than one that doesn't.

Dilution: why an ownership percentage isn't fixed

Dilution is the mechanic every equity holder needs to understand: as a company raises more money by issuing new shares, everyone's existing percentage ownership shrinks, even though the number of shares they personally hold doesn't change — the pie gets sliced into more pieces. This isn't necessarily bad for the equity holder in absolute terms: a smaller percentage of a company that's now worth much more, because that new funding enabled real growth, can still be worth far more in dollar terms than a larger percentage of a company that stayed small. The number that actually matters is the dollar value of the stake, not the percentage in isolation — a distinction that trips up a lot of early-stage equity holders who fixate on defending their ownership percentage rather than the company's total growth trajectory.

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.
