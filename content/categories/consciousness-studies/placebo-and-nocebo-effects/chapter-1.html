Give a patient a sugar pill and tell them it's a powerful painkiller, and a meaningful share of them will report real pain relief — not just claim it, but show measurable physiological changes, including the release of the body's own natural painkillers, endorphins (the body's internally produced pain-relieving and mood-boosting chemicals), detectable in brain scans. This is the placebo effect, and its existence is not remotely controversial. What's still being actively worked out is how far it reaches, and exactly what's driving it.

Not "just" belief — a real physiological pathway

Researcher Erik Vance's Suggestible You traces the modern understanding of placebo away from the old, dismissive framing ("it's not real, they just believed it would work") toward something more precise: expectation itself can trigger measurable biological cascades. Expecting pain relief activates the brain's own opioid system, detectably, on brain scans — and blocking that system with an opioid-blocking drug measurably reduces the placebo effect's pain-relieving power, direct evidence that a real physical pathway, not just a psychological one, is doing the work. Placebo effects have also been shown for measurable outcomes that are much harder to fake with sheer belief alone, including immune markers and hormone levels.

The mirror image: nocebo

The nocebo effect is placebo's darker twin: expecting harm can produce real harm. Patients warned about a drug's side effects report those exact side effects at meaningfully higher rates than patients given the same drug without being told what to expect — even when the "drug" is an inert placebo in the control group. In one striking category of cases, people who believe they're sensitive to something harmless (a placebo "electromagnetic field," for instance, in a genuinely blinded test) report real, measurable symptoms when they believe the exposure is happening, and no symptoms when it's secretly turned off — a serious complication for medicine, since properly informing patients of real risks can itself measurably increase how often those risks show up.

What placebo can't do

It's important not to overstate this: placebo effects are real and physiologically genuine for subjective and stress-influenced outcomes (pain, nausea, mood, some immune markers) — but they don't shrink tumors, heal broken bones, or cure infections. The effect works on the body's own regulatory and perceptual systems, not on physical damage or pathogens directly. This is exactly why placebo-controlled trials remain the gold standard in medicine: a treatment has to outperform this real, biologically active baseline to be considered genuinely effective, not just comforting.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Notice nocebo in your own reactions: next time you read a medication's side-effect list right before taking it, or feel worse right after being warned "this might sting," notice whether the warning itself might be shaping what you feel next.
  • Notice ordinary placebo effects: pay attention to how you feel a few minutes after taking a real medication you trust, before it could plausibly have acted yet — a low-stakes way to catch expectation working in real time.
