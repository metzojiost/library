In a well-known experiment, children who already enjoyed drawing were split into two groups. One group was told they'd get a reward — a certificate — for drawing. The other group drew with no reward mentioned at all. Weeks later, with no reward on offer either way, the children who had been paid drew significantly less than before, in their free time, than the children who'd never been paid. The reward hadn't added motivation. It had quietly replaced it.

The overjustification effect

Psychologists call this the overjustification effect: when an external reward is introduced for an activity someone already found enjoyable on its own, the reward can crowd out the original enjoyment, leaving behind only the reward as the reason to keep doing it — and once the reward disappears, so does the motivation. This is one of the central findings behind Daniel Pink's book Drive, which argues that traditional "carrot and stick" incentives — routine in schools, workplaces, and parenting — work reasonably well for simple, mechanical tasks, but can actively backfire on anything requiring creativity, judgment, or genuine engagement.

Self-Determination Theory: three ingredients, not one

The deeper research framework behind this is Self-Determination Theory, developed by psychologists Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, which proposes that sustained, high-quality motivation depends on satisfying three specific psychological needs: autonomy (feeling like you're acting from your own choice, not being controlled), competence (feeling genuinely capable and improving, not just going through motions), and relatedness (feeling connected to other people, not isolated in the activity). The theory draws a sharp distinction between intrinsic motivation (intrinsic = coming from within — doing something because it's inherently interesting or satisfying) and extrinsic motivation (extrinsic = coming from outside — doing something for a separate reward or to avoid punishment). External rewards aren't inherently bad — but when they undercut a person's sense of autonomy (a reward that feels like a bribe or a leash) rather than supporting their competence (a reward that feels like genuine recognition of skill), they tend to erode intrinsic motivation instead of adding to it.

Mindset: a related, but more contested, idea

Pink's book and Carol Dweck's Mindset are often shelved together, and the ideas do connect — Dweck's research proposes that people hold either a fixed mindset (believing ability is essentially set in stone) or a growth mindset (believing ability develops through effort), and that this belief itself changes how people respond to failure and challenge. It's worth being upfront that this is genuinely contested territory: several large-scale replication attempts in the 2010s found the effect of "growth mindset" interventions to be real but considerably smaller than the original, hugely popular claims suggested, and some effects didn't replicate cleanly at all. The underlying idea — that beliefs about ability shape behavior — is reasonably well supported; the size and reliability of specific classroom interventions built on it is the part still being actively debated.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Notice the overjustification effect in your own past: think of something you once enjoyed freely that turned into a paid job or graded task — did your enjoyment of it change, and in which direction?
  • Try the three-need check on a new habit: before starting something you want to stick with, ask whether it feels like your own choice (autonomy), has visible progress (competence), and ideally involves another person (relatedness).
