Chapter 1 established that knowledge requires justification, not just true belief. But that raises an immediate follow-up problem that's just as old: what justifies your justification? If belief A is justified by belief B, what justifies B? If B needs C, and C needs D, you're either running an infinite chain no one could ever actually complete, going in a circle, or stopping somewhere arbitrary. Philosophers call this the regress problem, and the two classic solutions to it define one of epistemology's oldest fault lines.

Foundationalism: stopping the chain on purpose

Foundationalism solves the regress by insisting it doesn't actually go on forever — it bottoms out in a special category of basic beliefs (beliefs that are justified without needing support from any other belief — self-justifying or justified directly by experience itself). Candidates for basic beliefs typically include immediate sensory experience ("I am currently seeing something red") or simple logical truths ("2+2=4"). Every other belief you hold gets justified by ultimately tracing back, through a chain of reasoning, to beliefs like these. The appeal is obvious: it gives justification a solid floor instead of an endless staircase. The difficulty is agreeing on what actually qualifies as basic. Even "I am seeing something red" turns out to be less bulletproof than it looks — it already presupposes concepts like "red" and "seeing" that themselves seem to require justification from somewhere.

Coherentism: no floor, just a web that holds itself up

Coherentism rejects the idea that any belief needs a special foundational status at all. Instead, a belief is justified by how well it coheres (fits together consistently and mutually supportively) with the rest of your entire belief system — justification isn't a one-way staircase from foundations upward, it's a web, where beliefs support each other in a mutually reinforcing structure, and no single belief needs to stand alone. On this view, asking "what's the ultimate foundation" is itself the wrong question — much like asking which single brick is holding up an arch, when the honest answer is that the arch's stability comes from how all the bricks are arranged together, not from any one of them individually.

The standard objection to each

Coherentism's most famous problem is the isolation objection: a system of beliefs can be perfectly internally consistent and still be completely disconnected from reality — an elaborate, self-consistent conspiracy theory or an internally coherent work of fiction can cohere beautifully with itself while being entirely false. If coherence alone is the test, nothing in the theory obviously rules that out, since coherence measures internal fit, not correspondence to the actual world. Foundationalism's most famous problem, sometimes called the arbitrariness objection, is nearly a mirror image: if basic beliefs are justified without needing support from other beliefs, what makes that justification any different from just asserting them, since by definition nothing is backing them up? Both camps have spent decades building more sophisticated versions of their positions specifically to blunt these two objections — modest foundationalism allows basic beliefs to be only weakly, defeasibly justified rather than certain; modest coherentism adds constraints requiring some contact with observation, not pure internal consistency alone. Neither patch has fully satisfied critics on the other side.

Why this isn't just academic hairsplitting

The regress problem shows up the moment anyone asks you to defend a belief past a couple of layers of "why." Ask someone why they trust a news source, then why they trust the reasoning behind that trust, then why they trust that — most conversations run out of patience long before they run out of possible follow-up questions, not because the chain resolves, but because everyone tacitly agrees to stop somewhere. Foundationalists and coherentists are really offering two different accounts of what's happening, and what's justified, at the exact moment everyone stops asking "why" and treats something as settled enough to build on.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Pick a belief you hold confidently and keep asking yourself "why" until you genuinely can't answer anymore — notice whether you land on something that feels foundationally self-evident, or whether it starts circling back to other beliefs you already hold.
  • Next time you catch an internally consistent but suspicious explanation (a conspiracy theory, an excuse, a sales pitch), test it against the isolation objection: is its consistency actually connected to outside evidence, or just internally tidy?
