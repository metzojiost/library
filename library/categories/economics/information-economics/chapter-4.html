Chapter 4: Moral Hazard — What Changes Once You're Covered

~5 min read

The first three chapters all shared a hidden structure: the private information existed before the deal was made — a car's condition, a candidate's ability, an applicant's risk level — and the whole challenge was getting that pre-existing fact revealed. This chapter is about a genuinely different problem: information (or more precisely, an action) that only becomes hidden after the deal is signed, because the deal itself changed what the informed party is now incentivized to do. Economists call this moral hazard (a change in behavior that occurs because a person is now insulated from some of the consequences of that behavior, in a way the other party can't fully observe or verify), and it's worth its own chapter because the fixes for it are almost entirely different from signaling or screening.

The term's own origin gives away the mechanism

The phrase comes directly from the insurance industry, and the original examples are literal: a homeowner with fire insurance has slightly less reason to be careful about fire risk than an uninsured one, because insurance absorbs part of the cost of carelessness. A renter driving a rental car tends to treat it, on average, a bit less carefully than their own car, because the rental company — not the driver — bears more of the cost if something goes wrong. Neither case requires bad faith. It's a rational response to a rational fact: when someone else is bearing part of your downside, your own incentive to avoid that downside is, mechanically, smaller than it would be if you bore all of it yourself. The insurer or lessor can't fully observe or verify the day-to-day care being taken, which is exactly what makes it a genuine information problem rather than just a contract-enforcement one.

Adverse selection versus moral hazard: same family, different timing

It's easy to blur this with Chapter 1's adverse selection, so the distinction is worth making precise: adverse selection is about hidden type, known before the deal, that the informed party has no incentive to disclose (a high-risk driver applying for insurance without volunteering their driving record). Moral hazard is about hidden behavior, occurring after the deal, that the deal itself made less costly to the person doing it (an already-insured driver becoming slightly less careful). One is about who you're dealing with; the other is about how the deal changes what they do next. Health insurance famously has to manage both at once — insurers screen and price for pre-existing risk (adverse selection) while also worrying that generous coverage reduces incentives for policyholders to avoid unnecessary utilization (moral hazard) — which is a large part of why health insurance contracts are as structurally complicated as they are.

The principal-agent problem: moral hazard's everyday name

Economists Michael Jensen and William Meckling formalized a closely related, broader version of this in 1976: the principal-agent problem (a situation where one party, the principal, hires another, the agent, to act on their behalf, but the agent's own incentives don't fully line up with the principal's, and the principal can't perfectly monitor the agent's effort or choices). A memorable empirical case comes from economists Steven Levitt and Chad Syverson's study of real estate agents: agents selling their own homes, on average, leave them on the market longer and sell for a meaningfully higher price than they achieve for otherwise-comparable client homes — because a standard commission structure gives the agent very little extra reward for the extra weeks of work it takes to hold out for a higher price on someone else's house, but the agent captures the full benefit when it's their own. Same expertise, same market, same person — different incentive structure produces a measurably different outcome. Corporate management has the identical shape at larger scale: a hired CEO's incentives don't automatically match shareholders' interests unless the compensation structure is deliberately built to align them.

How moral hazard actually gets managed

Because the problem is misaligned incentive after the fact, not hidden pre-existing type, the fixes look different from signaling or screening — they generally work by putting some of the cost back on the party whose behavior is hard to observe. Insurance deductibles and co-pays are the direct case: making the insured party bear a real slice of any claim restores some of their incentive to avoid the loss in the first place, even though it can't be perfectly monitored. Equity compensation for executives (rather than pure salary) ties the manager's own financial outcome to the company's performance, narrowing the gap between what's good for them and what's good for shareholders. Efficiency wages (paying somewhat above the market rate specifically so that losing the job would be a real cost worth avoiding) work by making the employee's own future income partly conditional on continued good performance, raising the personal stake in not slacking off even when direct monitoring is imperfect. None of these fully solve the problem — they manage it, by making sure the person with the private information also bears at least part of its cost.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Think of a situation where you (or someone you know) is at least partly insulated from the downside of a choice — a warranty, an expense account, a guaranteed outcome, a safety net — and notice honestly whether behavior shifted at all once that insulation was in place.
  • Look at your own compensation or incentive structure, at work or elsewhere, and ask what it actually rewards — effort, output, tenure, something else — versus what the person paying you actually wants, the way the real estate agent example shows those two things quietly diverging.
