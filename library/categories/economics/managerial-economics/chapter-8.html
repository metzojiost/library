Chapter 8: Time as the Scarce Resource

~3 min read

Every tool covered so far — marginal analysis, breakeven, economies of scale, sunk costs, transaction costs — has been stated mostly in terms of money. This chapter makes the case that the actually-binding scarce resource in most personal and even business decisions isn't money at all. It's time, and it needs its own dedicated economic treatment.

Becker's household production theory

Economist Gary Becker, in a landmark 1965 paper, made a case that seems obvious once stated but changed how economists think about non-market decisions: a household isn't just a unit that spends money on finished goods, it's a small production operation in its own right, combining time and purchased goods together to produce what actually gets consumed — a meal is produced from grocery goods plus cooking time, a clean home from cleaning supplies plus cleaning time, a raised child from money spent plus a large amount of parenting time. Once time is treated as a genuine input to production, not just a background condition, the same managerial-economics tools from every earlier chapter in this topic — opportunity cost, marginal analysis, economies of scale — apply directly to it.

The "full price" of anything

Becker's framework leads to a concept worth using deliberately: the full price of any activity or purchase is its money cost plus the value of the time it takes, valued at whatever that time's best alternative use would have been (Chapter 1's opportunity cost, applied specifically to time). A "cheap" $8 meal that takes two hours to cook has a full price that includes those two hours' opportunity cost — which might make a $15 meal that takes fifteen minutes the actually cheaper option overall, once time is honestly priced in, for someone whose time has a high opportunity cost in that specific hour. This isn't an argument that cooking is always the wrong choice — the cooking time might itself be valuable or enjoyable, which is a real benefit that belongs on the other side of the ledger — it's an argument for pricing time honestly rather than treating it as free just because no cash changes hands, echoing Chapter 1's original point directly.

Why this reframes "time management" advice

Most popular time-management advice treats time as something to be scheduled efficiently. Becker's framework treats it as something to be allocated — the same underlying question managerial economics asks about money and staff, just applied to hours: given a fixed daily supply of time, which uses of it actually produce the most value, accounting honestly for what each use's full price and full benefit really are? This reframing tends to surface real tradeoffs that pure scheduling advice misses entirely — not "how do I fit this in," but "is this actually the highest-value use of this specific block of time, once its full price is honestly counted."

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Pick one recurring activity you do to "save money" and calculate its full price: the money saved, minus (or plus, if you enjoy the activity itself) the value of the time it costs at your own honest opportunity cost. Does it still come out ahead?
