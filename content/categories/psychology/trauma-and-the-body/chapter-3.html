Chapter 1 mentioned, in passing, that brain scans during a triggered flashback show reduced activity in Broca's area — the region responsible for putting experience into words. That single finding is part of a much larger, well-documented picture of how trauma changes specific, identifiable brain structures and stress hormone systems. This chapter goes through that picture directly.

The amygdala: an alarm system stuck on high alert

The amygdala is a small, almond-shaped structure that acts as the brain's threat-detection alarm, rapidly flagging potential danger before slower, more deliberate brain regions can weigh in. Brain imaging studies of people with PTSD consistently show amygdala hyperactivation — a stronger, faster, more easily triggered alarm response than in people without trauma histories, firing even in response to cues that are only vaguely reminiscent of the original threat. This gives a direct biological account of the neuroception Chapter 2 described: an overactive amygdala is quicker to flag ambiguous cues as dangerous, tipping the nervous system out of the window of tolerance more readily and with less provocation than an untraumatized nervous system requires.

The prefrontal cortex: the brakes that don't engage in time

Under normal conditions, the prefrontal cortex — the brain's center for reasoning, planning, and emotional regulation — exerts a moderating, top-down influence on the amygdala's alarm signal, helping distinguish real danger from a false alarm and calm the response accordingly. In PTSD, imaging studies consistently find reduced prefrontal cortex activity during triggered states, meaning the brain's braking system is less available exactly when it's needed most. This is the same basic mechanism behind the Broca's area finding from Chapter 1 — Broca's area sits within a broader network of prefrontal-adjacent regions that go quiet during a flashback, which is why survivors so often report a genuine, physical inability to find words for what's happening to them in that moment, not a reluctance to talk about it.

The hippocampus: why a memory can feel like it's happening now

The hippocampus plays a central role in placing memories into proper context — encoding not just what happened, but when and where, which is what normally lets a memory be recalled as something that occurred in the past. Chronic stress hormone exposure, particularly elevated cortisol (the body's primary stress hormone, released by the adrenal glands as part of the fight-or-flight response), has been shown in multiple studies to be associated with measurably reduced hippocampal volume in people with PTSD. A hippocampus that isn't functioning at full capacity struggles to properly timestamp a traumatic memory as belonging to the past, which offers a biological explanation for why a flashback so often doesn't feel like remembering something — it feels like the event happening again, right now, because the neural machinery that would normally tag it as "then, not now" isn't doing its job reliably.

Beyond the brain: the body's stress response staying switched on

Chapter 1's hook about chronic pain and autoimmune conditions connects to the HPA axis (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis — the body's central stress-response system, coordinating cortisol release across the brain and body), which in PTSD often shows dysregulation persisting long after any actual threat has passed — sometimes chronically elevated, sometimes, counterintuitively, abnormally suppressed, depending on the specific trauma pattern and individual. Sustained HPA axis dysregulation has documented links to inflammation, immune function changes, and elevated risk for a range of physical health conditions, giving a real biological mechanism (not a vague mind-body metaphor) for van der Kolk's clinical observation, cited in Chapter 1, that unresolved trauma shows up as physical illness with no other obvious cause. The stress response isn't purely psychological; it's a body-wide hormonal and immune signal that can remain active on a hair trigger years after the triggering event has ended.

What this adds up to, and what it doesn't

Taken together, these findings support van der Kolk's central clinical claim from Chapter 1 with real biological specificity: trauma isn't simply a bad memory that talking it through should, in principle, straightforwardly resolve — it's a measurable, three-part shift in threat detection, emotional regulation, and memory-timestamping, plus a body-wide hormonal aftermath, and every one of those systems operates well below the level of conscious, deliberate control. It's worth being precise about the limits here too: these findings describe group-level patterns from brain imaging studies, not a diagnostic brain scan that could identify PTSD in any single individual with certainty — the biology explains why trauma feels the way it does and resists pure talk-based resolution; it doesn't offer a simple technological shortcut around the actual work of treatment.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Next time a strong reaction to something small catches you off guard, try silently naming which part of this picture might be active — an overactive alarm, an underactive brake, a memory not correctly timestamped — rather than just judging the reaction as excessive.
  • Notice a physical symptom with no clear cause in your own life or someone close to you, and hold it a little more loosely as possibly stress-related — not to dismiss it, but as a genuine biological possibility worth taking seriously alongside other explanations.
