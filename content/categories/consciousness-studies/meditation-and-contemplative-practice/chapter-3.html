Chapter 1 flagged, honestly, that meditation apps and popular coverage rarely mention that intensive practice can surface difficult, even distressing experiences — a real, under-discussed phenomenon sometimes called the "dark night" in contemplative circles. This deserves direct, serious treatment, both because it's now getting genuine clinical research attention, and because leaving it out entirely would badly misrepresent the actual, full picture this topic has been trying to give.

The study that took this seriously for the first time

Psychologist and contemplative researcher Willoughby Britton led the most systematic investigation of this territory, published as The Varieties of Contemplative Experience in 2017, based on in-depth interviews with meditation teachers, practitioners, and clinicians across multiple Buddhist traditions specifically about difficult and challenging experiences arising from meditation practice. The study documented a genuinely wide range of reported difficulties — ranging from mild, temporary disorientation to severe, functionally impairing episodes involving intense fear, dissociation, altered perception, and in some cases symptoms overlapping meaningfully with clinical conditions like depersonalization or psychosis-like states — occurring specifically in the context of intensive meditation retreats and sustained practice, including among experienced practitioners with substantial prior meditation history, not only complete beginners.

Why this doesn't fit cleanly into standard clinical categories

Britton's research team found something that complicated straightforward clinical classification: many of the reported experiences didn't map neatly onto standard psychiatric diagnostic categories, and many of the practitioners experiencing them weren't previously identifiable as at elevated psychiatric risk beforehand. Some difficult experiences appeared to be a documented, even historically named part of the contemplative path itself in some traditions — the term "dark night" originates from the 16th-century Christian mystic St. John of the Cross's own writing about a difficult period of spiritual practice, and various Buddhist traditions have their own distinct terminology for challenging phases (some describing specific stages involving fear, disgust, or a sense of dissolution as a recognized, if difficult, part of progressing through certain meditative insight practices) — while other reported experiences appeared more consistent with meditation acting as a genuine trigger or destabilizing factor for an underlying vulnerability, rather than a traditionally recognized stage of practice at all.

Why intensive retreat settings specifically carry more risk

A consistent pattern across this research is that risk concentrates disproportionately in intensive, prolonged retreat settings — many consecutive hours of daily meditation over days or weeks, often with reduced sleep, social isolation, and limited outside support — rather than in the kind of brief, daily practice most casual meditators, including the ones described in Chapter 1's clinical mindfulness research, actually do. This distinction matters directly for how to weigh this chapter against the rest of the topic: the well-controlled clinical research covered in Chapter 1, generally involving modest daily practice over weeks, has not documented anything close to the same rate or severity of adverse experience found in Britton's intensive-retreat-focused research — meaning the risk profile genuinely differs by dose and format, not just by individual vulnerability.

What this means for reading the whole topic accurately

Taking this chapter seriously doesn't require abandoning Chapter 1's real, well-supported positive findings — it means holding a more complete, more honest picture than either "meditation is a risk-free wellness practice" or "meditation is dangerous" would separately suggest. The responsible, evidence-consistent position, and the one increasingly reflected in how retreat centers and clinical mindfulness programs are adapting their own screening and support practices, is that meditation is a genuinely powerful intervention on the mind — powerful enough to produce Chapter 1's measured benefits and powerful enough, in a meaningful minority of cases, particularly under intensive conditions, to produce real difficulty requiring real support. That combination — genuinely effective and genuinely capable of producing adverse effects — is exactly the profile of any sufficiently powerful intervention on a complex system, and treating meditation as uniquely risk-free was always more a marketing framing than a research-supported one.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • If you're considering an intensive retreat, ask directly what support and screening the retreat center offers for difficult experiences, rather than assuming intensive practice carries the same low-risk profile as a brief daily sit.
  • Notice how differently this chapter and Chapter 1 read when placed side by side — and consider why a complete, accurate picture of any powerful practice usually needs both the benefits and the real risks stated plainly, not just one or the other.
