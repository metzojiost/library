Chapter 1 flagged a genuinely odd trend as one of the field's open puzzles: placebo response rates in clinical trials have been climbing for decades, particularly in the United States. This isn't a minor statistical curiosity — it's become a serious practical problem for drug development, because a rising placebo baseline makes it progressively harder for a genuinely effective new drug to demonstrate a large enough advantage over placebo to succeed in trials.

The trend, and why it's specifically an American pattern

A widely cited 2015 analysis by researcher Jeffrey Mogil and colleagues, examining decades of chronic pain trials, found that placebo response rates rose significantly between 1990 and 2013 — and that this rise was concentrated specifically in trials conducted in the United States, with much weaker or absent trends in trials run in Europe and elsewhere during the same period. This geographic specificity is itself an important clue: whatever is driving the rise isn't a universal biological shift in how human bodies respond to placebo, since that would show up everywhere equally. It points instead toward something about the trial environment, culture, or patient population that differs between the U.S. and other regions.

Leading candidate explanations

Several explanations have been proposed, and researchers generally treat them as plausibly overlapping rather than mutually exclusive. One candidate is direct-to-consumer pharmaceutical advertising, legal in the United States and New Zealand but banned in most other developed countries — the theory holds that constant exposure to confident, reassuring medication advertising may raise patients' baseline expectations of relief before they ever enter a trial, and Chapter 1 and Chapter 2 already established that expectation itself is a measurable driver of the effect. A second candidate is trial design itself: as chronic pain trials have grown larger, longer, and more elaborate, with more frequent clinician contact and more attentive care built into the trial protocol, that increased attention and care — the same "clinical ritual" Chapter 2 identified as doing real work in open-label placebo — may itself be inflating the placebo response independent of any actual drug.

A third candidate: who volunteers for trials

A further explanation involves possible shifts in the population of people who volunteer for clinical trials over time — if trials have gradually become more attractive to patients with milder, more suggestible, or more expectation-responsive symptom profiles (for instance, due to changes in compensation, recruitment methods, or how trials are marketed to potential participants), the average placebo response of the volunteer pool could rise for reasons that have nothing to do with expectation psychology changing at all, but rather with who ends up in the room. Disentangling this from the advertising and trial-design explanations is difficult with existing data, and most researchers in the area, including Mogil, treat this as a genuinely open question rather than a solved one.

Why this is a serious problem for medicine, not just a curiosity

The practical stakes are significant: a rising placebo baseline in exactly the trial-design context (U.S. chronic pain trials) where a large share of new analgesic drugs are tested means some genuinely effective drugs may be failing trials not because they don't work, but because the bar they need to clear over placebo keeps rising. Pharmaceutical companies have responded by exploring methodological countermeasures — including running trials in regions with historically lower placebo response, or using specific trial designs intended to reduce placebo response inflation — though no fully agreed-upon fix yet exists. This is a good example of a theme worth carrying forward from Chapter 2: placebo isn't a nuisance variable to be dismissed, but a real, measurable, and in this case genuinely consequential force actively shaping how modern medicine gets developed and evaluated.

Try this

Ideas for noticing or testing this in your own life.

  • Notice pharmaceutical advertising the next time you see it — pay attention to how confidently relief is portrayed, and consider Mogil's hypothesis that this kind of repeated exposure could be quietly raising a population's baseline expectations.
  • Consider the trial-attention explanation the next time you interact with any healthcare provider: notice how much of feeling cared for, independent of any specific treatment, might itself be doing therapeutic work.
